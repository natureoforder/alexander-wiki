# The Origins of Pattern Theory: the 1996 OOPSLA keynote

Keynote talk · ACM OOPSLA conference, San Jose, California, October 1996 · published as "The Origins of Pattern Theory: The Future of the Theory, and the Generation of a Living World", *IEEE Software* 16(5), September/October 1999, pp. 71–82, with an introduction by James Coplien

## What it is

Alexander's keynote to the leading conference on object-oriented programming, given at the invitation of the software community that had adopted his patterns. He describes how his own thinking had moved from *A Pattern Language* to the work that became *The Nature of Order*, why he now saw patterns as only a partial answer, and what he hoped programmers might take from the deeper theory of living structure and generative process.

## Why it matters

It is Alexander's own account of the relation between his early and late work, given to the audience that did most to spread his name outside architecture. It is also a challenge to that audience: he asks whether software patterns actually make programs that are better for people, or only better-organised code.

## Rufus's notes

> All my life I have spent trying to create living structure in the world

Rufus's view is that Alexander tends to be reduced in the software world to two ideas: be agile (iterate, don't fix a blueprint up front) and use patterns (ready-made templates for standard problems). The heart of his work is in *The Nature of Order*:

- generative processes for building living structure (crudely: identify centers and strengthen them);
- at each step, the *objective* criterion of whether a change increases the felt wholeness and life of the thing, a claim that is deliberately provocative to postmodern sensibilities;
- and the recognition that working this way conflicts with many systems of the modern world: planning, for example, demands a finished design up front, which is fundamentally at odds with a generative approach.
