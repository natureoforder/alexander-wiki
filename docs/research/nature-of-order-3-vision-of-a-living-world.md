## 2. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Preface: Living Processes Repeated Ten Million Times (pp. 1–20) and Author's Note: Freshness and Deep Adaptation (pp. 21–24)

Book 3 shows what the built world looks like if the living processes of Book 2 are used everywhere, by millions of people, at every scale. All such processes are sequences of one "fundamental process", restated here in seven steps (p. 4): see the whole, find its latent centers, strengthen the one that most increases the life of the whole, and with it a larger center, an adjacent equal center and smaller centers within, then start again. He stresses it is not a formula to recite and that every project needs its own invented process. The Author's Note adds the book's working test: buildings fall into two morphological classes, Class 1 (deeply adapted, nearly everything built before about 1900) and Class 2 (not deeply adapted, most published work of 1950–2000), and living process inevitably yields Class 1 whatever the style or materials. Examples: St Mark's Square over eleven centuries (pp. 5–7), the Leerdam drinking glasses (pp. 8–9), the Mexicali gateway built by families (pp. 10–11), Emoto apartments Tokyo (pp. 11–12), Mountain View Civic Center courtyard (pp. 13–16), West Dean Visitor's Centre (pp. 16–17).

> "One deep class of processes — infinitely varied — does everything." (p. 4)

> "For it is not style that makes a building living or dead, but the freshness of its response to its surroundings; the truthful and spontaneous unfolding of order within its own fabric." (p. 22)

### Part One (p. 26) and Chapter 1: Belonging and Not-Belonging (pp. 27–40)

The most important human issue in the built environment is belonging: the sense that the world is ours and we have a right to be in it. Twentieth-century development destroyed it by making private places anonymous and identical and by taking public space away from the people who use it. Belonging needs three things, and only living process produces them: a unique private world for each person or family, a public world that works as "the living room of society", and continuous touching of the two. The precondition throughout is that people control their own environment. Examples: Prague faces, November 1989 (pp. 27–29); Economist building London, an Oakland office block, a Pasadena motel and a Florida public housing project as not-belonging (pp. 30–36); the village compound of Bavra, Gujarat, where he lived in 1961 (pp. 34–35, 38); four family doorways at East Wahdat, Amman (pp. 37–38); the homebase street at Eishin (p. 39).

> "In traditional society, up until the onset of this century, the space that existed between buildings was like the living room of society." (p. 34)

> "True belonging — true life — occurs when a penetration of the real into the fabric of the world occurs." (p. 38)

### Chapter 2: Our Belonging to the World (pp. 41–66)

Belonging is shown rather than argued: a set of informal photographs and his own paintings of people at ease, which he calls "true comfort" or "the blissful state". The claim is that this state depends on minute features of the physical surroundings, cannot be produced by design, planning or construction "in the accepted sense", and is the real test of architecture. The fifteen properties underlie it, above all NOT-SEPARATENESS, ROUGHNESS and THE VOID; its geometric signature is fine grain, small local symmetries in a full hierarchy, and a rubbed-in, imperfect quality (wabi-to-sabi). Examples: a French housing project undercroft versus a lawn with cartwheels (p. 44); the yard of old building materials and ducks (p. 45); a street in Shanghai (p. 46); a Japanese restaurant near Eishin (p. 48); his father in a deck-chair on Downland turf (p. 50); his wife Pamela in their kitchen (p. 58); his workstation keyboard on a cardboard box (p. 58); his paintings *Caribbean light*, *The Living Room*, *The blue cupboard* (pp. 59–62).

> "The true landscape of architecture is just that condition which gently supports, steers into existence, this subtle condition, and which is to be judged, entirely, on its actual performance, its actual ability to nourish us — its track record if you like — in helping us to be ourselves." (pp. 54–57)

> "Living process which succeeds in making belonging always leads to fine grain" (p. 64)

### Part Two opener (p. 68)

Five chapters on land and building form share one theme: "the positive character of living space" is "the single most important and most tangible result of living processes at work" (p. 68).

### Chapter 3: The Hulls of Public Space (pp. 69–100)

Living process applied to a town first produces a connected skeleton of positive, partly enclosed, mainly pedestrian outdoor spaces, which he names "hulls" (German *die Hülle*). Space is shaped first, by judgment on the ground and in space-only models; buildings come afterwards and exist to form it. He revises his earlier position: purely piecemeal growth is not enough, and a town needs a shared three-dimensional diagram of its hulls that individual projects then complete. Codes, zoning and master plans cannot do this, and transport engineers must become subsidiary to the hulls. Examples: Nolli plan of Rome and its black-white reversal (pp. 73–74); LIFT street theatre in London (p. 71); Progresso district, Fort Lauderdale (p. 76); the Eishin campus spine of streets, gates, lake and bridge (pp. 77–81); Back of the Moon, Austin, where a latent common land was staked before houses were placed (pp. 82–85); Parkstadt-Unterliederbach, Hoechst, Frankfurt, with its cardboard space model, street-width experiments in Oakland and a twelve-step sequence (pp. 86–92); San Martino al Cimino (p. 93); the Samarkand competition drawing (p. 94); Agate housing, Eugene (pp. 95–96).

> "I choose to call these coherent, partly enclosed public spaces that are positively shaped, the HULLS of public space." (p. 70)

> "Pure piecemeal growth just does not work well enough to create the structure of the larger wholeness needed in a city." (p. 93)

### Chapter 4: Large Public Buildings (pp. 101–152)

A large public building unfolds from the hull it heads: it is the "jewel" whose volume is found by asking what will most intensify the feeling of the public space, and it stays human through an unbroken pyramid of scales from the whole volume down to ornament, every part and every space between parts a living center or "being". The second half is the book's main statement on procedure. Life in a big building needs users at the core, engineering and money integrated with design from the first day, a fixed-price contract in which the design floats and the architect-builder moves money and changes detail during construction without change orders, craft subcontracts held directly, and constant full-size mockups. He concedes that for the very largest projects the right process has "barely yet" been imagined (p. 120). Examples: Great Hall at Eishin (pp. 102–111); Mountain View Civic Center, unbuilt (pp. 112–115); Tokyo International Forum competition, four feeling-led steps (pp. 115–119); Eurostar terminal, Waterloo (p. 119); Julian Street Inn, San Jose, with the twelve-item memo to the client on grilles, tiles, capitals, trusses and arches (pp. 120–131); Mary Rose Museum, Portsmouth, in eight processes, with West Dean as its test bed (pp. 132–150); Nyingma monastery, Kathmandu (pp. 151–152).

> "Everything you make must be a being." (p. 103)

> "What floats is the design, not the price." (p. 144)

### Chapter 5: The Positive Pattern of Space and Volume in Three Dimensions on the Land (pp. 153–190)

Siting is the most consequential step in any building process. Each new volume must fit the existing configuration of centers almost like a molecular lock and key, preserving the centers already there and strengthening latent ones, so that building and outdoor space become positive together. The work is done on the land itself, with stakes and flags, and in rough models; the phase ends with three deliverables: the building staked out, a 1:200 clay topographic model, and light-card volumes on it. For a larger project two systems of centers must be fused, the generic ones named by the users' pattern language and the ones already in the land. A volume that lacks feeling at this stage cannot be rescued later, and the buildings are only tools for bringing life to the land. Examples: Otto Wagner's pump house on the Danube canal (pp. 155–156); Church of the Holy Trinity sketch on the Thames (pp. 156–158); Berryessa house among white oaks (pp. 159–165); Emoto apartment building, Tokyo, in fifty steps (pp. 166–173); Eishin campus site plan, its balsa model and two hundred flags (pp. 173–181); Agate and Amazon student housing, University of Oregon (pp. 182–186); the Sacramento students and the "sausage of baker's dough" (p. 189).

> "IN ORDER TO PRESERVE THE STRUCTURE OF THE LAND, THE NEW CENTERS COMING FROM THE PATTERN LANGUAGE MUST BE ESTABLISHED IN SUCH A WAY THAT THEY FALL NATURALLY IN PLACES THAT COINCIDE WITH, OR ENHANCE, THE NATURALLY OCCURRING CENTERS OF THE SITE." (p. 175)

> "IT IS THE LAND WHICH MATTERS. The purpose of the buildings is to bring life to the land." (p. 188)
