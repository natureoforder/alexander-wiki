## 2. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Author's Note and Preface: On Process (pp. xiv–xviii, 1–14)

Book One defined living structure statically; Book Two asks what kind of process can produce it. Alexander's claim is that process, not design, mostly decides whether a building lives, and that the fragmented, goal-driven modern building process (programme, drawings, bid, assembly of catalogue parts, zoning) cannot produce life whatever the architect's talent. Living and "unfolded" structure will turn out to be the same thing seen statically and dynamically. Examples: Bill McClung's meadows in the Berkeley hills; a 15th-century Hispano-Moresque tile whose design is "a residue" of the rope-in-clay technique; San-ju-san Gen Do, Kyoto; the steelworker who placed the last beam on the Empire State Building.

- "Architects are much too concerned with the design of the world (its static structure), and not yet concerned enough with the design of the generative processes that create the world (its dynamic structure)." (p. 4)
- "In a good process, each person working on the building is — and feels — responsible for everything." (p. 13)

### Part One: Structure-Preserving Transformations (pp. 15–16)

The opener states the thesis of chapters 1–4: a living process "always has enormous respect for the state (and morphology and form) of what exists" and finds a next step that preserves and extends its latent structure (p. 16). Chapters 1–2 argue this for nature, 3–4 for the built world.

### Ch 1. The Principle of Unfolding Wholeness in Nature (pp. 17–50)

Nature produces living structure, with the fifteen properties, effortlessly; why? Alexander proposes a "principle of unfolding wholeness": every natural system moves forward in the way that preserves and intensifies the existing system of centers and symmetries, destroying as little as possible, so that each state grows smoothly out of the one before even through catastrophes. Existing explanations (local mechanics, least action, non-linear dynamics, Darwinian selection) each explain single cases but not why the fifteen properties recur. The law is "temperamental": humans can violate it, which is why modern building fails. Offered as "proto-mathematics" (p. 20). Examples: about twenty natural sequences, including spiral galaxy M51, frog embryo, breaking wave (Thom), milk-drop splash, Belousov-Zhabotinski reaction, crystal growth, slime mold, shattering glass, mouse foot, Shepherd's Purse seed, snow crystals, Penrose quasicrystals, the bombardier beetle.

- "This principle states that, in the evolution of an otherwise undisturbed system, the wholeness W is progressively enhanced and intensified." (p. 22)
- "As the system evolves, it destroys these symmetries and larger centers AS LITTLE AS POSSIBLE." (p. 46)
- On architecture: if living structure always arises "by successive transformations of what exists", then "the view of the unfettered architect-creator that has been fostered in the last four hundred years, must be completely wrong." (p. 48)

### Ch 2. Structure-Preserving Transformations (pp. 51–84)

Defines the book's central operation. A structure-preserving transformation intensifies a center already latent in the existing wholeness, so it is at once conservative and innovative; repeated, it yields dense living structure, and one structure-destroying step in a sequence is very hard to recover from. Whether a step preserves structure is claimed to be objective (laboratory experiments with octagons and rectangles, early 1980s). The key move comes in section 8: the fifteen properties of Book One are recast as fifteen transformations, a "limited palette" of the only ways a wholeness can be extended. Value itself becomes the degree to which unfolding has respected what exists. Examples: the D1–D7 circle sequence; square and square-with-dot; two trees and a hammock; the Tokyo apartment building at a street fork (1987); two mailboxes in the Berkeley hills; buckling cylinder and dew on a spider's web (minimum symmetry breaking); milk-drop splash; a mountain under "millions of transformations".

- "Thus the procedure is both conservative (it respects the previously existing structure) and innovative (it creates new structure, not previously visible in the older structure of D1)." (p. 53)
- "We see now that the fifteen properties are not merely observable end-products of structure-preserving transformations. They provide the base transformations from which, in practice, all structure-preserving transformations are made." (p. 80)

The fifteen transformations as he glosses them (pp. 77–79): levels of scale (adds intermediate-sized centers); strong center ("the most fundamental transformation of all", p. 77); boundary; alternating repetition; positive space; good shape; local symmetry; deep interlock; contrast; gradient; roughness; echoes; the void ("getting rid of garbage", p. 79); simplicity (removes unneeded centers); not-separateness ("a kind of knitting", p. 79).

### Ch 3. Structure-Preserving Transformations in Traditional Society (pp. 85–106)

Traditional building had life because its processes, like nature's, were structure-preserving at every step: no design on paper, a known sequence of steps, and constant feedback so that each part is fitted to the emerging whole. The same holds from a log to a city built by "hundreds of millions of actions" over centuries. For a person (unlike nature), preserving structure requires paying attention to the whole, which Alexander equates with love of life. Examples: the Samoan canoe song ("First find your tree", p. 86); Blackfoot tipi; Mali wall painting; a 15th-century textile pattern and a Tabriz carpet; adzed logs of Norwegian and Swedish houses; Austrian villages; Italian doorway steps; the Duomo and plan of Florence; Amsterdam 1400–1800; the Mausoleum of Mama Khatun, Tercan (fourteen of fifteen properties); a Rio favela play space; a Turkish family's veranda.

- "Any part of the world we build will have life if it is created by structure-preserving transformations, and will not have life if it is not created by structure-preserving transformations." (p. 86)
- "The modern process leaves no room for feedback." (p. 92)
- "This 'paying attention to the wholeness' is essentially synonymous with love of life." (p. 103)

### Ch 4. Structure-Destroying Transformations in Modern Society (pp. 107–136)

Unfolding is not automatic for humans, who act from mental images and schemata that may or may not fit the wholeness that exists. Since about 1900 the normal processes (planning bureaucracy biased "toward the gigantic", loan finance, regulation, mass production, the master plan itself) have been structure-destroying, both towards surroundings and within a building's own geometry. Architects were "pawns" who then glamorised the result; and image-driven design is in principle incompatible with unfolding, because any preconceived image dissolves if taken step by step through real adaptation. Examples: the freeway through Providence; Le Corbusier-era slab housing slashed through Algiers and its casbah (1940–80); a Pasadena street and a condominium over parking; Sydney Harbour bridge versus Sakura bridge, Tokyo; a desert tract; two radial settlements on the Amazon; a Southern California stucco bank with rounded corners; the San Francisco Marriott; Botta's cylindrical house; Wright's Guggenheim; Le Corbusier's Marseille block and La Tourette.

- "Paying back the loan becomes the underlying process, and the buildings which are created become artifacts charged mainly with the achievement of the loan." (p. 116)
- "An unfolding process, by its nature, produces things that are alive. The image-driven process, by its nature, produces things which are dead." (p. 132)
- "In a living system what is to be always grows out of what is, supports it, extends its structure smoothly and continuously, elaborates new form — sometimes startling new form — but without ever violating the structure which exists." (p. 136)

### Interlude and Ch 5. Living Process in the Modern Era (pp. 137–174)

A deliberate counterweight to chapter 4 ("Passionately, I love the modern era", p. 138): living process still occurs in the 20th century, usually where nobody thought the matter important enough to control. Each case gets a rough bulleted "generating process", the book's first written sequences. What they share is roughness, directness and freedom from concepts: one practical thing is done, then the next. Examples: Matisse and his birds; Geoffrey Bawa's house in Sri Lanka; lower Manhattan around Wall Street; St Paul railroad yards; planting daffodils at Meadow Lodge, Sussex; a customised Harley; Times Square in the rain; the Golden Gate Bridge; chairs in the Luxembourg Gardens; ping-pong on a Shanghai sidewalk; the TGV nose; Wright's Coonley and Dana house plans; Lilia Duran's house, Mexicali (1976, $3,500); the Upham house; a Yogyakarta community centre; the river edge at Belém; Guatemala squatter housing; Chinese terraced fields.

- "What looks like an informal, rambling process is in reality a more highly organized process than any currently practiced by architects or planners." (p. 174)
- "If we do one thing at a time, and if what we do is wholesome and sound, then whatever comes next will work." (p. 174)

### Part Two: Living Processes (pp. 175–178)

The opener turns from the principle to its working features, and introduces the generative/descriptive distinction through a long quotation from Lewis Wolpert's *Principles of Development* (origami: folding instructions versus a description of the folded bird). Chapters 6–17 then list what any living process must have.

- "Generative processes tell us what to DO, what ACTIONS to take, step by step, to make buildings and building designs unfold beautifully, rather than detailed drawings which tell us what the END-result is supposed to be." (p. 176)
- "Living structure in buildings can only be GENERATED. It cannot be created by brute force from designs." (p. 177)

### Ch 6. Generated Structure (pp. 179–202)

All successful complex systems are generated, not fabricated, and the difference is measurable as mistakes of adaptation. Every line in a plan is a decision that is right or wrong for its context; a drawing-board plan cannot correct them, a process unfolding in time and in the hands of the people concerned can. He estimates about 5,000 decision points in a typical house, a million possible mistakes in a 150-house community, and puts a money value on them (a tenfold difference in a house's worth). Complex adaptation comes by differentiation of a whole, not addition of parts, with continual simplification to keep room for more relationships. Examples: seven settlement plans from Indore, India (Shilnath and two others generated; Doshi's and three others fabricated); one Shilnath house in seven overlapping relationships; a blue porch versus a Fort Lauderdale tract porch; his own library (about 1,000 decisions in twelve days); a Japanese tea bowl versus a Royal Dutch wine glass; software (note 2 cites Beck and Cunningham on extreme programming); a 6th-century BC Greek marble horse.

- "ALL the well-ordered complex systems we know in the world, all those anyway that we view as highly successful, are GENERATED structures, not fabricated structures." (p. 180)
- "This must be done in sequence and in context because the necessary information for a successful decision is not available prior to that step in the unfolding." (p. 201)
- Differentiation "is a process of dividing and differentiating a whole to get the parts, rather than adding parts together to get a whole." (p. 197)

### Ch 7. The Fundamental Differentiating Process (pp. 203–226)

The pivot of the book. A living process is defined as "any adaptive process which generates living structure, step by step, through structure-preserving transformations" (p. 204). Every act is both an individual process (one centre made from start to finish) and part of an accretive process (the larger whole built by many hands with no completion date). All living processes are chains or nested hierarchies of one repeated step, the fundamental process (p. 216), in eleven clauses: (1) take the structure as it now is; (2) attend to its wholeness; (3) find where it is weakest as a whole, "most deeply lacking in feeling"; (4) look for latent centres; (5) choose one; (6) differentiate and strengthen it with one or more of the fifteen transformations; (7–8) new centres appear and larger, parallel and smaller centres are strengthened; (9) test that life really increased; (10) test that this is "the simplest differentiation possible"; (11) repeat, stopping when no further step intensifies the whole. The chapter closes with ten necessary features of living process, which are the titles of chapters 8–17 (p. 225). Examples: Guatemalan terrace farmer; masons and the town of Acca, Yemen; the Sanders house, Sonoma, unfolded in seven steps each labelled with the transformations used; Tucson as failed accretion; Bill McClung's meadow-making procedure in his own words.

- "The rules of the game — the way architects, builders, planners, painters, gardeners, and ordinary citizens play their roles and undertake their actions — that is what controls the form of the world." (p. 215)
- "Do one small good thing; then do another small good thing; then do another good thing." (p. 218)

### Ch 8. Step-by-Step Adaptation (pp. 229–248)

First necessary feature: a living process goes gradually, with feedback at each step, and so its end-state cannot be fixed in advance. The geometry of unfolded things (a falling water drop, Nolli's Rome) cannot be drawn; it can only be produced by a history of small corrected steps. Ashby's thirty coins show why: tossed all at once they take centuries to come up all heads, one at a time about a minute. Drawings give no real feedback about how a building feels ("a polite lie on which our 20th-century architecture was based", p. 245); rough cardboard models, mock-ups on site and decisions made during construction do. Examples: the daffodil assembled by micro-tweezers; the front doorstep tried out with blocks; windows placed from inside the framed room; the Mountain View and Eishin Great Hall paper models; the George house stair; eight film stills of Matisse painting *Woman in a Chair* (1946).

- "So, to work well, a living process must not merely be step by step. It must be step by step with a built-in feedback of such a kind that each step taken can be checked at once for the increase of life which will occur, accepted if it has it, rejected if it does not." (p. 237)
- "But when adaptation and feedback are working, the result must be unpredictable." (p. 240)

### Ch 9. Each Step Is Always Helping to Enhance the Whole (pp. 249–266)

Second feature: what each step does is enhance the larger whole, not perfect a part. The whole that guides is not a future plan but the present configuration together with its latent centres; strengthening those both preserves and extends. For early design he proposes working in "morphological ripples": global, fuzzy features settled one at a time, held in words and the mind's eye on the real site, because a sketch forces too many arbitrary decisions too early. Examples: St Mark's Square, Venice, 560–1600, shown as ten cycles of context / latent centres / action / new construction (pp. 252–254); the rejected Pasadena zoning rule; a Berkeley hills canyon talked through with Bill McClung; his painting of a cargo ship in the Port of Oakland.

- Draft rule for Pasadena, 1988, struck out after the planning chairman asked if he was a Communist: "Any proposed new apartment building must help the life of the street and of the neighborhood in some tangible way" (p. 250)
- "In developing these latent centers, one is then both respecting the structure which exists, yet also paving a path to some as-yet unborn new structure." (p. 255)
