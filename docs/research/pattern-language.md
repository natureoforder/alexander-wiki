# A Pattern Language (1977): research digest

Source: Christopher Alexander, Sara Ishikawa, Murray Silverstein et al., *A Pattern Language: Towns, Buildings, Construction* (Oxford University Press, 1977; "volume 2" of the Center for Environmental Structure series). Built from `natureoforder/sources/texts/pattern-language/markdown/` (front matter, Using this Book, the three part introductions, the 253 pattern files) and Rufus's notes in `natureoforder/pattern-language/` (README.md, shortlist.md). Page numbers are printed pages (roman in the front matter). Quotes are verbatim from the Markdown, including its `---` for dashes. This digest covers the structure and use of the language, not a summary of the patterns.

## 1. The book in brief

253 patterns, each a recurring problem and the core of its solution, ordered from regions down to construction details. It is the "sourcebook" half of a pair: "This book provides a language, for building and planning; the other book provides the theory and instructions for the use of the language" (p. ix), the other being *The Timeless Way of Building*. The claim there is that towns and buildings come alive only when everyone makes them within a shared, living language; this book offers "one possible pattern language" distilled from eight years of building and planning (p. x).

For the wiki the point is that the book is a tool with a method. The patterns form a network read as a sequence; each carries a confidence rating; you select a small subset for your project, add your own, and apply it with a different procedure at each of three scales. Alexander later treated pattern languages as the first stage, followed by sequences and generative codes (section 6).

## 2. The structure of the language

### 2.3 How a pattern is built

Every pattern has one format (pp. x-xi): a picture; an intro paragraph naming the larger patterns it completes; three diamonds; a bold headline stating the problem; the body (evidence, variants); the bold solution after "Therefore:"; a diagram; three diamonds; a closing paragraph naming the smaller patterns that complete it. The solution "is always stated in the form of an instruction---so that you know exactly what you need to do, to build the pattern" (p. xi). Pattern 60 shows it. Problem (pp. 304-305): "People need green open places to go to; when they are close they use them. But if the greens are more than three minutes away, the distance overwhelms the need." After "Therefore:" (pp. 308-309): "Build one open public green within three minutes' walk---about 750 feet---of every house and workplace."

Two purposes: "to present each pattern connected to other patterns, so that you grasp the collection of all 253 patterns as a whole, as a language", and to present problem and solution "in such a way that you can judge it for yourself, and modify it" (p. xi). Wiki pattern pages should keep these elements.

### 2.4 How patterns connect

Each pattern "is connected to certain 'larger' patterns which come above it in the language; and to certain 'smaller' patterns which come below it" (p. xii): larger ones on its first page, smaller on its last. The book's case is ACCESSIBLE GREEN (60): larger 13, 14, 41, 59; smaller Positive Outdoor Space, TREE PLACES (171), GARDEN WALL (173) (pp. xii-xiii; the intro misprints Positive Outdoor Space as 107, it is 106).

> "Each pattern can exist in the world, only to the extent that is supported by other patterns: the larger patterns in which it is embedded, the patterns of the same size that surround it, and the smaller patterns which are embedded in it." (p. xiii)

The ethic: "when you build a thing you cannot merely build that thing in isolation, but must also repair the world around it, and within it, so that the larger world at that one place becomes more coherent, and more whole" (p. xiii). From the files, a pattern averages about 3.4 references to larger and 5.2 to smaller patterns (regex on "(NNN)", approximate).

### 2.5 Network and sequence

"A pattern language has the structure of a network... However, when we use the network of a language, we always use it as a sequence, going through the patterns, moving always from the larger patterns to the smaller, always from the ones which create structures, to the ones which then embellish those structures, and then to those which embellish the embellishments" (p. xviii). "Since the language is in truth a network, there is no one sequence which perfectly captures it" (p. xviii). The printed sequence is summary, index and "base map".

### 2.6 Star ratings

Two, one or no stars (★★, ★, none in the titles). "The solutions we have given to these problems vary in significance. Some are more true, more profound, more certain, than others" (p. xiv).

- Two stars: "we believe that we have succeeded in stating a true invariant"; "it is not possible to solve the stated problem properly, without shaping the environment in one way or another according to the pattern that we have given" (p. xiv).
- One star: "we believe it would be wise for you to treat the pattern with a certain amount of disrespect---and that you seek out variants of the solution" (p. xiv).
- None: "we are certain that we have not succeeded in defining a true invariant" (pp. xiv-xv).

The frame is scientific: "each pattern may be looked upon as a hypothesis like one of the hypotheses of science" and "no matter what the asterisks say, the patterns are still hypotheses, all 253 of them---and are therefore all tentative" (p. xv). Stars rate the solution's confidence, not its importance. Rufus's seven VERY IMPORTANT patterns (75, 76, 77, 105, 106, 111, 114) show the gap: only 105 and 106 are two-star. The wiki should label stars "confidence" and keep project importance separate.

### 2.7 Making a project language

"Choosing a Language for Your Project" (pp. xxxv-xl) starts from the premise that "any small sequence of patterns from this language is itself a language for a smaller part of the environment" (p. xxxv). The example is ten patterns (140, 161, 163, 167, 120, 190, 212, 242, 245, 251): "one of a thousand possible languages for a porch, at the front of a house" (p. xxxvi). One author used it on his own house, enlarging the 6 ft balcony to 12 by 12 ft for two conversation areas and cutting the platform corner to keep the existing path. "The character of what you build, will be given to it by the language of patterns you use, to generate it" (p. xxxvii). The eight steps (pp. xxxviii-xl):

1. Copy the master sequence "on which you can tick off the patterns which will form the language for your project".
2. "find the pattern which best describes the overall scope of the project you have in mind. This is the starting pattern for your project."
3. Read it. "The ones at the beginning will tend to be 'larger' than your project. Don't include them, unless you have the power to help create these patterns, at least in a small way, in the world around your project. The ones at the end are 'smaller.' Almost all of them will be important."
4. Repeat from the next ticked pattern; "do not tick the ones which are 'larger' unless you can do something about them, concretely, in your own project."
5. "When in doubt about a pattern, don't include it."
6. Continue until done.
7. "adjust the sequence by adding your own material": "there is no pattern for a sauna. If you want to include one, write it in somewhere near BATHING ROOM (144)."
8. Change patterns you disagree with, and "change the name of the pattern too".

Steps 3 and 4 (take smaller patterns freely, larger ones only if you can act on them) is what scales the starter sets in section 3.

### 2.8 Using the language at each scale

"Patterns dealing with towns can only be implemented gradually, by grass roots action; patterns for a building can be built up in your mind, and marked out on the ground; patterns for construction must be built physically, on the site" (p. xl). Fuller procedures are in *The Timeless Way* (town ch. 24-25, building 20-22, construction 23).

**Towns (pp. 3-7).** "We do not believe that these large patterns, which give so much structure to a town or neighborhood, can be created by centralized authority, or by laws, or by master plans. We believe instead that they can emerge gradually and organically, almost of their own accord, if every act of building, large or small, takes on the responsibility for gradually shaping its small corner of the world to make these larger patterns appear there" (p. 3). Groups from region (8,000,000) to "House clusters and work communities, with 30-50 people each" (p. 4) each own the common land at their own level. "Implementation should be loose and voluntary as far as possible, based on social responsibility and not on legislation or coercion" (p. 5). Individual projects can start early, for example neighbours who "tear up the asphalt, and build a GREEN STREET there instead" (pp. 6-7).

**Buildings (pp. 463-466).** Thirteen steps. "Take the patterns in the order of the sequence, one by one, and let the form grow from the fusion of these patterns, the site, and your own instincts"; "above all, work on the site, stay on the site, let the site tell you its secrets" (p. 463). "Don't rush this process. Don't give the form more order than it needs" (pp. 463-464). "Stand on the site with your eyes closed. Imagine how things might be, if the pattern, as you have understood it, had suddenly sprung up there overnight" (p. 464), then mark it with string, cardboard and stakes. "Complete your thought about this pattern, before you go on to the next one" (p. 464); later changes "will get smaller and smaller... like a series of progressive refinements" (p. 464). Keep a budget from the start: "Keep track of the area from the very beginning so that you are always reasonably close to something you can actually afford" (p. 465). "Try not to design on paper; even in the case of complicated buildings find a way to make your marks on the site" (p. 466).

**Construction (pp. 935-938).** For "professional builders, or amateur owner-builders" (p. 935). "except for the first four patterns, which deal with structural philosophy, the remaining patterns can actually be used, in the sequence given, to build a building" (p. 936). The patterns are "both more concrete, and more abstract, than any other patterns" (p. 936), each with one buildable example (Mexico, Peru and Berkeley systems, p. 937) but open to remaking. The aim: "an alternative to the technocratic and rigid ways of building that have become the legacy of the machine age and modern architecture" (pp. 935-936).

### 2.9 Status, and the poetry

"in a healthy society there will be as many pattern languages as there are people" (p. xvi), so the title stresses "A". But the authors claim "at least a part of the language we have presented here, is the archetypal core of all possible pattern languages", naming ARCADES (119) and ALCOVES (179) as ones no valid language could omit (p. xvii). A closing note warns against mechanical use: "The difference between prose and poetry is not that different languages are used, but that the same language is used, differently" (p. xli). Dense buildings overlap many patterns in one space, as when BATHING ROOM (144) merges with STILL WATER (71) in a shared pool (pp. xlii-xliii).

## 3. Patterns by scale for Build with Alexander

These starter sets follow the book's procedure (2.7): a starting pattern, then its smaller patterns, larger ones only where you can act. Each is in book order, because the order is what makes the language work. How to run any set is in 2.8: stand on the site, one pattern at a time, stakes and string, a budget in square feet, a loose design. "Rufus" is his `shortlist.md`, 49 patterns for the Petit Bois Martin hamlet: 37, 75-77, then 96-168; VERY IMPORTANT are 75, 76, 77, 105, 106, 111, 114; IMPORTANT are 110 and 147; "listed" is on the list unmarked. He has nothing from Construction and omits hubs these sets add (95, 104, 163, 179, 180, 190, 191, 205). Pages are first printed pages. "Why" is my paraphrase.
