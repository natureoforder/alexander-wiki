## 1. The book in brief

Book One sets out what Alexander thinks order is. It argues that "life" is not confined to organisms but is present in some degree in every region of space, that people largely agree about its degree when they consult feeling rather than opinion, and that it has a definite structure. That structure is wholeness: a field of centers, each made of other centers, which have more life the more they help one another. Fifteen recurring geometric properties describe the ways centers help each other; they recur in the best human work and throughout nature. Part Two adds that this structure is personal: things with more life are better "pictures of the self", observers can measure life by the wholeness it induces in them, living structure supports human freedom, and function and ornament are one phenomenon. It ends by proposing that space itself awakens where centers form.

In the four-book series this is the static, descriptive volume. It replaces the "quality without a name" and the individual patterns of the 1970s books with one structure, and leaves how living structure is made to Book 2, built examples to Book 3, and the metaphysics of self and matter to Book 4.

## 2. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Prologue to Books 1–4 (pp. 1–4) and Preface (pp. 5–24)

The prologue frames all four books: building is an order-creating process as important as those of physics and biology, yet we have no adequate idea of what order is. Alexander presents himself as a reluctant metaphysician and "hard-boiled empiricist" driven by the practical wish to make beautiful buildings, and names the two cosmological claims of the series: all matter/space has some degree of life (Book 1) and some degree of self (Book 4). The preface diagnoses twentieth-century architecture as a "mass psychosis" caused by a mechanist-rationalist world-picture traced to Descartes, which removed the "I" and removed value from what can be true. His remedy is an extended idea of truth in which statements about relative life or wholeness are factual. Examples: the Tower of the Wild Goose ("the yellow tower"), Ise shrine columns, the tomb of Timur, the Berlin Palm House, set against Amsterdam post-war apartments, Wright's Marin County Civic Center and Saarinen's Milwaukee War Memorial; the door-in-a-wall example of two kinds of statement; Fuller, Le Corbusier and Graves as one-value architecture; the oil-drum house.

- "One of these is the claim that all space and matter, organic or inorganic, has some degree of life in it, and that matter/space is more alive or less alive according to its structure and arrangement." (p. 4)
- "In the world-view I am presenting, a second kind of statement is also considered capable of being true or false. These are statements about relative degree of life, degree of harmony, or degree of wholeness — in short, statements about value." (p. 17)

### Part One opener and Chapter 1, The Phenomenon of Life (pp. 27–62)

The ecological wish to build towns that are "alive" has no scientific footing, because biology defines life only for self-reproducing organisms. Alexander proposes a broader conception in which every thing and every region of space has some degree of life, with organic life a special case. The chapter does no proving: it collects shared intuitions that we feel degrees of life in non-organic systems, organisms, ecosystems, human events, traditional and modern artifacts, and in poverty, and asserts that the same structure lies behind all of them. Examples: southern England as a 300-mile man-made living structure; the breaking wave; gold, Carrara marble, the moons of Jupiter; the leaping cheetah; a back-street Japanese restaurant; the Minoan vase, the Isfahan mosque, the Kizaemon tea bowl; a Bangkok slum house set against a postmodern house; ducks in his Berkeley garden.

- "And what I mean, in general, is that every single part of the matter-space continuum has life in some degree, with some parts having very much less, and others having very much more." (p. 31)
- "These things are all beautiful, but they are all damaged. Life itself is damaged, and nothing which is perfect can be truly alive." (p. 60)

### Chapter 2, Degrees of Life (pp. 63–78)

Life is a matter of degree, present even between a printed full stop and a letter q, and people largely agree about which of two things has more of it when they answer from feeling rather than theory. Alexander walks through photograph pairs (more life on the left) and reports classroom tests, then argues that resistance to the question comes from the mechanistic world-picture and from the threat it poses to architectural fashion. He closes with the book's fundamental hypothesis. Examples: Dominica street against a Harlem slum; road with trees against road with traffic lights; ordinary pickup truck against the "organic" painted car (the image of life is not life); two parking lots fifty feet apart at Berkeley; the 1992 lecture where 89 of 110 architecture students chose the Bangkok slum house, 21 abstained and none chose the postmodern octagonal tower; the illuminated manuscript against a postmodern auditorium wall; the Yurok naming of rocks and trees (T. T. Waterman); the Nagoya low-rise housing survey (note 4).

- "What we call "life" is a general condition which exists, to some degree or other, in every part of space: brick, stone, grass, river, painting, building, daffodil, human being, forest, city." (p. 77)
- "… every part of space — every connected region of space, small or large — has some degree of life, and that this degree of life is well defined, objectively existing, and measurable." (p. 77)

### Chapter 3, Wholeness and the Theory of Centers (pp. 79–108)

The chapter supplies the book's two technical terms. The wholeness (W) of any region of space is a real, neutral, mathematically definable structure: the system of coherent entities in it, nested and overlapping, with their relative strengths. Those entities are called centers, not wholes, because they have no exact boundary and exist as focused zones within a larger field; they are induced by the configuration as a whole rather than assembled from pre-existing parts. Wholeness is global, fluid (a dot of 0.0001 of a sheet changes all of it), often cuts across the things we have names for, includes cultural and momentary human centers, and is the sole source of life. Examples: the dot on a sheet of paper and its twenty induced entities; the fishpond that cannot be bounded; Pamela and the bedroom curtains; arches A and B; the tree, road and bicycle (the "donut" of space under the tree); the family garden in West Sussex; Matisse's four self-portraits with different features and the same face; the two-slit electron experiment; light rays in Hagia Sophia; two boys talking in a shed; people sitting on the ground in India.

- "I use the word center to identify an organized zone of space — that is to say, a distinct set of points in space, which, because of its organization, because of its internal coherence, and because of its relation to its context, exhibits centeredness, forms a local zone of relative centeredness with respect to the other parts of space." (p. 84)
- "The flower is not made from petals. The petals are made from their role and position in the flower." (pp. 87–88)

### Chapter 4, How Life Comes from Wholeness (pp. 109–142)

Life is structural: a wholeness has more life when its centers help one another. Four ideas carry the argument: centers themselves have life; centers help one another; centers are made only of centers (a deliberately recursive definition, each center being a field of other centers); and a structure has life according to the density and intensity of its centers. "Living structure" is the name for a wholeness in which this mutual, bootstrap intensification is dense. The practical test for helping is to compare A with and without B. Number of centers is not the point (the "fallacy of baroque architecture"), and the effect depends on hair's-breadth accuracy. Examples: the terrace of the Hotel Palumbo at Ravello (chamfered columns helping the 13-foot bays, the end garden as turning point, the cheap light fitting); the apple tree and leaf; the Alhambra tile fragment built up in five steps; motel, Georgian and Nubian doors; a porch column intensified step by step; the Temple of Hera at Paestum; the Northumberland house against a Gwathmey house; the fruit-tree guild; Guatemalan fishermen mending a net; a girl throwing a ball; students in 1988 failing to copy the Ghirlandaio carpet border's hundred centers.

- "Centers are always made of other centers. A center is not a point, not a perceived center of gravity. It is rather a field of organized force in an object or part of an object which makes that object or part exhibit centrality." (p. 118)
- "All in all we have a bootstrap relation, in which no one center is the origin of the structure or its life — but the various different centers all support each other mutually." (p. 126)

### Chapter 5, Fifteen Fundamental Properties (pp. 143–242)

The longest chapter (100 pages) reports an empirical result: comparing thousands of pairs of things for two or three hours a day over twenty years, Alexander found about fifteen geometric features that recur in whatever has more life. He first held them as raw observations (identified 1966–73, well defined by 1976, in unpublished manuscripts 1975–85) and only around 1985 understood them as "the fifteen ways in which centers can help each other come to life" (p. 145). They overlap and partly define one another (interdependence matrix, p. 238), which is what led him to treat the field of centers, not the properties, as primary. The number is approximate ("not five, and not a hundred, but about fifteen", p. 242) and the list may not be exhaustive. Each property is taught by a positive and a negative example:

1. Levels of scale (pp. 145–150): Matisse drawing against an Albers painting; an old Irish door against a jig-cut eighteen-panel door.
2. Strong centers (pp. 151–157): the mosque of Kairouan against a Bruce Goff house; an Anatolian carpet whose field effect starts at the edge.
3. Boundaries (pp. 158–164): the Norwegian storehouse; the rule that a boundary must be of the same order of size as what it bounds (lips, arcade, the quays of the Seine).
4. Alternating repetition (pp. 165–172): Greek island embroidery against a modern office facade; the chintamani velvet.
5. Positive space (pp. 173–178): the Nolli plan of Rome; ripening corn; the space beside the Kizaemon bowl.
6. Good shape (pp. 179–185): the Korean teapot stand against a futuristic chair; good shape as a recursive rule built from simple elementary figures.
7. Local symmetries (pp. 186–194): the Alhambra plan against the Detroit Renaissance Center and Speer's Zeppelinfeld; his Harvard black-and-white strips experiment.
8. Deep interlock and ambiguity (pp. 195–199): the arcade that belongs to building and street; Tabriz mosque tilework.
9. Contrast (pp. 200–204): the Shaker schoolroom against a glaring modern lobby.
10. Gradients (pp. 205–209): window sizes up a facade; rills on a Persian glass bottle.
11. Roughness (pp. 210–217): the Persian sinekli bowl; the "patched" corner of a carpet border; Kairouan tiles against postmodern painted brickwork.
12. Echoes (pp. 218–221): Alberobello cones and a Turkish prayer carpet against a Michelangelo building he calls a "hodgepodge".
13. The void (pp. 222–225): the Ghiordes prayer rug; the mosque of Baybars against a 1970s office plan; cornfield, barn and duckpond.
14. Simplicity and inner calm (pp. 226–229): Shaker furniture.
15. Not-separateness (pp. 230–235): the Tower of the Wild Goose against an egocentric house; "perhaps the most important property of all".

- "I finally recognized that it is the field of centers which is primary, not these fifteen properties, and that the properties are simply aspects of the field which help us to understand concretely how the field works." (p. 238)
- "All the examples show how the seemingly rough solution — which seems superficially inaccurate — is in fact more precise, not less so, because it comes about as a result of paying attention to what matters most, and letting go of what matters less." (p. 211)

### Chapter 6, The Fifteen Properties in Nature (pp. 243–298)

To answer the "cognitive" objection (that centers and properties are only facts about human perception), Alexander shows each of the fifteen properties recurring in natural systems at every scale. Each instance has its own mechanical explanation, but no current theory explains why the same properties keep appearing; his suggestion is that they are the structural complements of stable systems, and that nature produces them because natural processes are structure-preserving (the full account is deferred to Book 2). The consequence is that all nature is living structure, that only human designers can step outside it, and that value is therefore a feature of nature, not an addition to it. Examples: zones of a river; electrical discharge (levels of scale); bubble rafts and crystal dislocations (roughness); thick boundaries with three unrelated causes (blood cell wall, the Rio Tapajós silt islands in the Amazon, the sun's corona); the sets C (all possible configurations) and L (living structure); the featureless space between Jupiter and Saturn; Love Canal.

- "Essentially, nature always follows the rule that each wholeness which comes into being preserves the structure of the previous wholeness, so that all of nature is just that structure which can be created by a smooth structure-preserving process of unfolding." (p. 293)
- "Not all nature is equally beautiful. Not all of it is equally deep in its wholeness. Some of nature may be "better" than other parts of nature." (p. 294)

### Part Two opener and Chapter 7, The Personal Nature of Order (pp. 297–312)

Part Two turns from structure to the person: living structure "is at once both structural and personal" (p. 298), which Alexander offers as a bridge across what Whitehead called the bifurcation of nature. Chapter 7 redefines "personal" as what touches our shared humanity, not what is idiosyncratic, and claims that the field of centers is present in a thing to the degree that it has personal feeling; feeling (distinguished from emotion) is the inward aspect of life, and we become happy in the presence of deep wholeness. He flags, as unproven, the larger theme of Book 4 that the universe is made of "person-stuff". Examples: a child's sketch-like drawing of the Dome of the Rock; van Gogh's boats; the meadow of wildflowers; the birthday table and cake read through the properties; wedding ring, handshake and namaste; three sheets of paper (diamond, blank, squiggle) and which you would give to someone you love; drawings of women by Picasso, Moore and Matisse; ocean waves and wild geese.

- "As the centers deepen, the personal feeling of the structure increases. If its personal feeling does not increase, its structure is not really getting deeper." (p. 308)
- "The external phenomenon we call wholeness or life in the world and the internal experience of personal feeling and wholeness within ourselves are connected. They are, at some level, one and the same thing." (p. 309)

### Chapter 8, The Mirror of the Self (pp. 313–350)

How is degree of life to be judged in disputed cases? Alexander's answer is an empirical test: of two things, ask which is the better picture of your whole self (good and bad, past and hoped-for), or which you would rather become by the day of your death. He reports 80–90 percent agreement across cultures and ages when the question is properly understood, and distinguishes it sharply from preference. "Liking from the heart" converges between people as they mature, can be checked by staying power over months, and coincides with living structure; knowledge of the fifteen properties sharpens the feeling rather than replacing it. Self here is not a formula: it is not the traditional, the complex, the informal or the ornamented. Examples: ketchup bottle against an old salt shaker; the 1985 Omega workshop where one man in a hundred chose the gray steel stool over the blue bench, and his later letter; small flowered cup against a large mug; axe against Phillips screwdriver; dime against nickel; Bill Huggins choosing the Ersari prayer rug he "didn't like" over the Daghestan; Mount Athos against Mies van der Rohe's Detroit apartments; Botta house against a Swedish cottage (1988 students); car repair shop against a "nice" restaurant; Hardwick Hall against a 1960s shingled house; the Eames house against a chocolate shop; a Seljuk tomb.

- "In the method of observation which I propose, the observer asks to what degree each of the two things we are trying to judge is, or is not, a picture of the self — and by this I mean your and my wholesome self, perhaps even our eternal self." (p. 316)
- "I assert, as a matter of fact, that the things which people truly and deeply like are precisely these things which have the mirror-of-the-self property to a very high degree." (p. 342)

### Chapter 9, Beyond Descartes: A New Form of Scientific Observation (pp. 351–370)

The mirror-of-the-self test (found in the late 1970s) is one of a family of tests in which observers report their own inner state of wholeness in the presence of two things; because different observers converge, the result is objective in the scientific sense of being shared, and it measures the thing, not the observer's satisfaction. Alexander calls this a second method of observation, complementary to Descartes's first: use the machine model where the facts are machine-like, and the observer's wholeness where relative wholeness is at issue. He notes precedents in Confucius, Socrates and the Buddhist *Visuddhimagga* (wholesome and unwholesome states), and admits the daily working question is plainer than the "exotic" mirror test. Examples: the list of nine alternative questions (life, harmony as in Aikido, devotion, closeness to God, expanding humanity); the homeless man on Telegraph Avenue pressing three fingers into his hand, then the credit-card transaction in the record store; an iron railing against a thermostat; Pennsylvania barn ornament against an ornament from Brasília; the Oakland Bay Bridge design debate.

- "Comparing A and B, which one makes me feel the most wholeness in myself, which allows me to come closest to my own life, which makes me experience life most deeply?" (p. 354)
- "Instead, to get practical results, we must use ourselves as measuring instruments, in a new form of measuring process which relies (necessarily) on the human observer and that observer's observation of his or her own inner state." (p. 354)

### Chapter 10, The Impact of Living Structure on Human Life (pp. 371–402)

Living structure matters practically because it supports inner freedom. Borrowing Max Wertheimer's definition (freedom is the ability to react appropriately to any circumstance), Alexander argues that the environment acts like a trace element: small unresolved conflicts built into buildings each add to one cumulative "stress reservoir", and when it overflows people are cut off from reality and from themselves. A living environment removes such conflicts (this is how he now reads the 253 patterns of *A Pattern Language*) and, beyond that, surrounds people with self-like structure that nourishes them. He rests the claim on what clients said about his own buildings. Examples: the sloping concrete wall at the Berkeley University Art Museum; the mother with small children on the sixth floor; Fort Lauderdale housing entered from the parking lot; a Terry Farrell building on the Thames; André Kertész's photographs of Paris; José Tapia at Mexicali; Ann Medlock's poem about her Whidbey Island house; the Eishin campus (the art student on NHK in 1991, the teachers' dream of walking by water, the lake, the students' 1989 film of jumping into it, Hosoi on secret places).

- "The perfectly free human being is a person who, no matter what she or he encounters, can act appropriately." (p. 374)
- "Each pattern, when examined carefully, describes some conflict — better said, some system of conflicting forces which will arise in the wrong sort of environment, but which can be tamed, resolved, when the environment is right." (pp. 380–381)

### Chapter 11, The Awakening of Space (pp. 403–444)

The closing chapter dissolves the split between function and ornament: both are the same field of centers, and function is the dynamic aspect of wholeness (transitory centers of use in harmony, or not, with the static centers of the form). Lists of goals or needs are arbitrary and regress without end; in practice the makers of the best things worked by making centers strong, and good function came with it, so "form follows function" has the order backwards. The basic rule of function becomes to make every center precious. From there Alexander restates the book's hypothesis as a claim about matter: space itself carries life in varying degrees, each center is a spot where space "awakens", and what awakens he will later call "the ground". He cites Diderot, Whitehead, Wigner, Buddhism and Imanishi as precedents and defers the explanation to Book 4. Examples: his own list of 390 requirements for rapid transit stations; the Japanese chisel; the living room (quiet core, end of a sequence, window place, fire) at Meadow Lodge and the Upham house kitchen; Hillier and Hanson's beady-ring villages; the fire-safe meadow in northern California; the fish pond; a front door with benches and plum trees; the visit to Tofuku-ji in 1967 and the blue dragonfly.

- "Function, like wholeness itself, is all based on centers. Function is simply the dynamic aspect of wholeness." (p. 405)
- "Thus life itself is a recursive effect which occurs in space. It can only be understood recursively as the mutual intensification of life by life." (p. 434)

### Conclusion (pp. 441–444) and Appendices 1–6 (pp. 445–472)

The conclusion restates the book as "a foundation for all of architecture": wholeness is a real mathematical structure in space, life springs from it, a shared criterion of judgment is the necessary core of any architecture, and Books 2 and 3 must show the "restricted" processes that can create living structure. The appendices are the technical backing: (1) a set-theoretic definition of W as subregions of a region R ranked by a coherence measure c between 0 and 1, the top-ranked being centers; (2) a worked seven-point example tied to the Harvard strip data; (3) the cognitive difficulty of seeing wholeness (educated Radcliffe students sorted strips by reading left to right, children by overall configuration); (4) the new kind of mathematical field needed, in which a center's intensity depends on its neighbours'; (5) the two-slit experiment read through Bohm; (6) Nikos Salingaros's 1997 measure L = TH applied to twenty-four famous buildings (Alhambra and Taj Mahal 90, Seagram 8, TWA terminal 6).

- "A criterion for judgment is the core — the necessary core — of any architecture." (p. 442)

## 5. Links to the other books

Stated in the text unless marked editorial.

- *The Timeless Way of Building*: he identifies the "ordinary but intensely living quality" of chapter 1 with what chapters 1–2 of *Timeless Way* described (p. 36), and says the fifteen properties elaborate observations in its chapter 23, written 1975 (ch. 5 note 1, p. 242). Editorial: "life" and "wholeness" here replace the quality without a name.
- *A Pattern Language*: reread twice. In the preface the patterns are the test case for value statements being true (pp. 17–18); in chapter 10 each pattern is a resolved system of conflicting forces that drains the stress reservoir (pp. 380–382); in chapter 11 the notes call the APL treatment "more functional", written before "the geometric-functional unity of space" was clear to him (note 3, p. 439), and reuse Intimacy Gradient, Corner Doors, Window Place, The Fire and Pools of Light as centers.
- Early work: the 390 rapid-transit requirements (p. 405) and the Harvard subsymmetry papers of 1964 and 1968 (p. 242) are cited as superseded and as evidence respectively; a burned 1965–67 manuscript, "The Universe of Forms", first tried to build all form from coherent wholes (ch. 3 note 12).
- Book 2, *The Process of Creating Life*: why twentieth-century processes fail to make life (p. 50); why the properties appear in nature, through structure-preserving unfolding (pp. 244, 292–294); wholeness as sequential unfolding of symmetries (Book 2 ch. 14, cited p. 242); how to act consistently with existing wholeness (Book 2 chs. 13–15, cited p. 453). Editorial: the properties return there as transformations.
- Book 3, *A Vision of a Living World*: the Mexicali and Eishin processes (p. 394) and the architecture based on living space (p. 425).
- Book 4, *The Luminous Ground*: the second cosmological claim, that matter/space has "self" (p. 4); the personal as "a vital substrate underlying matter" (p. 309); the link between self and matter (ch. 8 notes, citing Book 4 chs. 2, 3, 5, 9, 10); "the ground" and the "I" (pp. 425, 439).
- Outside the series: Nikos Salingaros's computable measure of life (app. 6); Whitehead's bifurcation of nature (pp. 298, 314) and Bohm on order and wholeness (pp. 10, 453, app. 5) are his main philosophical anchors.

## 7. Text quality notes

- Status: only the prologue and chapter 7 are reviewed; everything else is machine draft and every quote above needs a check against the scan.
- Column interleaving: two-column pages are often merged out of order, so sentences break mid-thought and resume a paragraph later (for example pp. 6–7, 28–29, 61–62, 85–86). Page attributions near a page turn can be off by one; figure-heavy pages (for example p. 381) have no text of their own.
- Dropped lines: short lines vanish, including list items and sentence openings (the list of the fifteen properties on p. 144 is cut off after the fifth; item 3 of the five assertions on p. 122 is partly lost; several section openings in chapters 3–6 start mid-sentence).
- Recurring character errors, silently corrected in quotes: "2oth", "zoth", "roth", "oth" for 20th; "igth" for 19th; "i5th" for 15th; "T" for "I" (p. 16); "1" for "I" as a pronoun; "ir space" (p. 28); "bebauror" for behavior (p. 95); "dor", "balo", "saliens" in the dot example (pp. 81–82); small caps rendered erratically ("GooD sHape", "sTRoNg CENTErs"); italics lost throughout, with italic f, h and th misread ("barmony", "buman", "bow").
- Dashes and spacing: em-dash spacing is inconsistent and some hyphens at line ends are fused ("lifeis", "helpfulhelpful"). Quotes above normalise to spaced em dashes.
- Unreadable matter: the Latin manuscript caption on p. 46 is noise; "wabito-sabi" (p. 60) should be checked against the scan; exponents are flattened ("2100" for 2 to the power 100, "27 = 128" on p. 449); footnote markers appear as stray punctuation ("?", "'", "!") after words.
- Stale cross-references in the printed text itself (not OCR): chapter 3 points to "function and ornament (chapter 12)" (p. 80); chapters 4 and 10 and appendix 6 call the function chapter "chapter 7" (pp. 134, 372, 469), which is now chapter 11; chapter 7 points to the mirror test "as in chapter 9" (p. 306), now chapter 8. Cite by chapter title, not by his internal numbers.
- Dates conflict slightly: the properties were "first identified" in 1966–73 (p. 237) yet observed from "about twenty years ago" and listed in manuscripts of 1975–85 (p. 144); the mirror test dates from "the late 1970s" (p. 353) and "about 1978" (p. 354).
