# The Timeless Way of Building (1979): research digest

Source: `natureoforder/sources/texts/timeless-way-of-building/markdown/` (machine draft of the Oxford University Press edition, 1979; 27 chapters in five parts, 549 pages of text). Page numbers are printed pages from each file's `page_starts`. Quotes are verbatim from that draft with obvious OCR slips corrected (section 7). Each chapter opens with an italic summary, and the body is threaded with italic "headlines" that Alexander says carry the whole argument ("If you read the beginning and end of every chapter, and the italic headlines that lie between them … you will be able to get the overall structure of the book in less than an hour", front matter, unnumbered). Italics in quotes below mark those summaries and headlines.

## 1. The book in brief

Volume 1 of the Center for Environmental Structure series: the theory behind *A Pattern Language* (volume 2) and *The Oregon Experiment* (volume 3). It argues that places which are alive share one objective but unnameable quality, a freedom from inner contradiction, and that this quality cannot be designed, only generated. Places are made of patterns: recurring relationships in space fused with recurring events. Patterns come from pattern languages that people carry in their minds, which work like a genetic code: shared, they let millions of small acts add up to a coherent, endlessly varied whole. Those languages have died in industrial society, so they must be rebuilt explicitly, pattern by pattern, tested against feeling. The second half shows the process: differentiating a whole one pattern at a time, building without drawings, repairing, growing a town piecemeal with no master plan. The last chapter withdraws the scaffolding: the language is only a "gate" to an egoless state in which it is no longer needed. It is the first full statement of the two questions *The Nature of Order* later answers differently: what life in a place is, and what process produces it.

## 2. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Part: The timeless way

Divider: "A building or a town will only be alive to the extent that it is governed by the timeless way." (p. 1)

**1. The timeless way** (pp. 3–16). Summary: "*It is a process which brings order out of nothing but ourselves; it cannot be attained, but it will happen of its own accord, if we will only let it.*" (p. 3). All building that lives, from villages to the Alhambra, comes from one invariant process, now identifiable through two ideas: patterns and the pattern languages that generate them. It is precise but not mechanical: it frees a capacity people already have and fear to use, so that "what this method does is simply free us from all method" (p. 13).

- "This is the timeless way of building: learning the discipline—and shedding it." (p. 16)

### Part: The quality

Divider: "To seek the timeless way we must first know the quality without a name." (p. 17)

**2. The quality without a name** (pp. 19–40). Summary: "*There is a central quality which is the root criterion of life and spirit in a man, a town, a building, or a wilderness. This quality is objective and precise, but it cannot be named.*" (p. 19). The difference between good and bad building is objective: a system being at one with itself rather than at war with itself. He circles the quality with seven words, each of which fits and then fails: alive, whole, comfortable, free, exact, egoless, eternal. Examples: the peach tree on the wall, the well-made fire, the bird table, the Japanese fishpond.

- "It is a subtle kind of freedom from inner contradictions." (p. 26)

**3. Being alive** (pp. 41–54). Summary: "*The search which we make for this quality, in our own lives, is the central search of any person, and the crux of any individual person's story. It is the search for those moments and situations when we are most alive.*" (p. 41). We know the quality first in ourselves, when we let go of images of how to live and our inner forces are resolved. So we can recognise it in places by asking "whether they are like us when we are free" (p. 53). Examples: Kurosawa's *Ikiru*, the high-wire artist, gypsies in the rain.

- "Places which have this quality, invite this quality to come to life in us. And when we have this quality in us, we tend to make it come to life in towns and buildings which we help to build." (pp. 53–54)

**4. Patterns of events** (pp. 55–74). Summary: "*In order to define this quality in buildings and in towns, we must begin by understanding that every place is given its character by certain patterns of events that keep on happening there.*" (p. 55). A place is what keeps happening in it, human and non-human; a whole life runs on about a dozen recurring patterns of events, inseparable from their space: the Bombay sidewalk and the New York sidewalk are two patterns, not one pattern with two uses. Examples: the reflecting pool versus the stream with a rowing boat, "watching the world go by" on a porch.

- "The action and the space are indivisible. … The two form a unit, a pattern of events in space." (p. 70)

**5. Patterns of space** (pp. 75–100). Summary: "*These patterns of events are always interlocked with certain geometric patterns in the space. Indeed, as we shall see, each building and each town is ultimately made out of these patterns in the space, and out of nothing else: they are the atoms and the molecules from which a building or a town is made.*" (p. 75). The apparent elements of a building (nave, aisle, door) differ every time; what repeats is relationships, and the elements themselves dissolve into patterns of relationships among smaller patterns. Each is a morphological law: "Within a context of type X, the parts A, B, ... are related by the relationship r" (p. 90). A building is defined by a few dozen patterns, a city by a few hundred. Examples: the gothic cathedral, freeway and cloverleaf, the porch, Venice, London.

- "And finally, the things which seem like elements dissolve, and leave a fabric of relationships behind, which is the stuff that actually repeats itself, and gives the structure to a building or a town." (p. 89)

**6. Patterns which are alive** (pp. 101–122). Summary: "*The specific patterns out of which a building or a town is made may be alive or dead. To the extent they are alive, they let our inner forces loose, and set us free; but when they are dead they keep us locked in inner conflict.*" (p. 101). A living pattern lets the forces in a situation resolve themselves; a dead one sets up conflicts nobody can resolve, leaving people in chronic stress. He then refuses the anthropocentric reading: patterns are good because they are stable and self-sustaining in themselves, like ripples in sand, not because they serve us. Examples: work mixed with family or separated, the living and the dead courtyard, the window place versus "holes in the wall", the Greek whitewashed band versus the Los Angeles café kept indoors by law.

- "In short, a pattern lives when it allows its own internal forces to resolve themselves." (p. 120)
- "Good patterns are good because to some extent each one of them reaches the quality without a name itself." (p. 116)

**7. The multiplicity of living patterns** (pp. 123–136). Summary: "*The more living patterns there are in a thing—a room, a building, or a town—the more it comes to life as an entirety, the more it glows, the more it has this self-maintaining fire, which is the quality without a name.*" (p. 123). Life belongs to systems of patterns. A dead pattern cannot contain its forces, which leak out and destabilise its neighbours; a living one needs both the larger patterns around it and the smaller ones inside it. Examples: the unbraced column–beam joint, WINDOW PLACE needing LOW WINDOWSILL and SMALL PANES, the whole entrance, the whole neighbourhood.

- "And finally the quality without a name appears, not when an isolated pattern lives, but when an entire system of patterns, interdependent at many levels, is all stable and alive." (p. 135)

**8. The quality itself** (pp. 137–154). Summary: "*And when a building has this fire, then it becomes a part of nature. Like ocean waves, or blades of grass, its parts are governed by the endless play of repetition and variety, created in the presence of the fact that all things pass. This is the quality itself.*" (p. 137). A place made of living patterns has a geometric signature, "the character of nature": the same patterns everywhere, yet every part unique because each meets different local conditions. This is the opposite of modular building; the geometry is loose, slightly irregular, and made in acceptance that the thing will age and die. Examples: waves and drops, atoms in a crystal, SUNNY PLACE turning a corner.

- "The repetition of patterns is quite a different thing from the repetition of parts." (p. 150)
- "The character of nature can't arise without the presence and the consciousness of death." (p. 153)

### Part: The gate

Divider: "To reach the quality without a name we must then build a living pattern language as a gate." (p. 155)

**9. The flower and the seed** (pp. 157–166). Summary: "*This quality in buildings and in towns cannot be made, but only generated, indirectly, by the ordinary actions of the people, just as a flower cannot be made, but only generated from the seed.*" (p. 157). Organic complexity cannot be designed from above; each part must adapt autonomously to local conditions. Autonomy alone gives chaos, so the parts need a shared code, as cells share a genetic code. Is there such a code for building? Examples: the Samoan canoe, the flower built with tweezers.

- "the great complexity of an organic system, which is essential to its life, cannot be created from above directly; it can only be generated indirectly." (p. 162)

**10. Our pattern languages** (pp. 167–192). Summary: "*The people can shape buildings for themselves, and have done it for centuries, by using languages which I call pattern languages. A pattern language gives each person who uses it, the power to create an infinite variety of new and unique buildings, just as his ordinary language gives him the power to create an infinite variety of sentences.*" (p. 167). Traditional barns are alike yet endlessly varied because the builder carries a system of patterns in his mind. Patterns in the mind, unlike patterns in the world, are generative rules with imperative force. A pattern language is a finite combinatory system in which patterns are both elements and rules. Examples: California barns, hillside terracing, the Bernese Oberland farmhouse, stone houses of southern Italy.

- "Each pattern is a rule which describes what you have to do to generate the entity which it defines." (p. 182)

**11. Our pattern languages (continued)** (pp. 193–210). Summary: "*These pattern languages are not confined to villages and farm society. All acts of building are governed by a pattern language of some sort, and the patterns in the world are there, entirely because they are created by the languages which people use.*" (p. 193). Freeways, gas stations and office towers also come from languages: handbooks, codes, bank lending rules. Architects make perhaps 5 percent of buildings (p. 199). Everyone, Palladio and Wright included, designs from the language in their mind, and language enables creativity as grammar enables speech. Examples: Stevenage, the AASHO highway manual.

- "Your pattern language is the sum total of your knowledge of how to build." (pp. 202–203)

**12. The creative power of language** (pp. 211–224). Summary: "*And, beyond that, it is not just the shape of towns and buildings which comes to them from pattern languages, it is their quality as well. Even the life and beauty of the most awe-inspiring great religious buildings came from the languages their builders used.*" (p. 211). Chartres was not designed at a drawing board; it was built by people steeped in one shared language. The deepest knowledge is ordinary and can be stated as simple rules; greatness lies in refusing to let any go. Examples: the hairline of a third colour in old Turkish prayer rugs, light on two sides, the Ise shrine.

- "Each pattern is a field—not fixed, but a bundle of relationships, capable of being different each time that it occurs, yet deep enough to bestow life wherever it occurs." (p. 223)

**13. The breakdown of language** (pp. 225–242). Summary: "*But in our time the languages have broken down. Since they are no longer shared, the processes which keep them deep have broken down: and it is therefore virtually impossible for anybody, in our time, to make a building live.*" (p. 225). Dead places come from languages too. A living language is shared, covers the whole of life and keeps users in direct touch with building. In industrial society languages became specialised, private and fragmented, people lost trust in their own feelings, and control from above made things worse. Examples: his university office and its nine patterns, the Berkeley seminar rooms, Le Corbusier's Marseilles block.

- "the central task of \"architecture\" is the creation of a single, shared, evolving, pattern language, which everyone contributes to, and everyone can use." (p. 241)

**14. Patterns which can be shared** (pp. 243–276). Summary: "*To work our way toward a shared and living language once again, we must first learn how to discover patterns which are deep, and capable of generating life.*" (p. 243). The formal definition: a pattern is at once a thing in the world and "the rule which tells us how to create that thing" (p. 247), a three-part rule, Context → System of forces → Configuration (p. 253), making two empirical claims that can be true or false. Patterns are found by sorting places that feel good from those that do not and seeking the invariant, or by argument. The core is a "morphological feeling", not a formula; to be usable it must be made a thing, drawn and named. Examples: the alcoves of Ostenfeldgaarden, ENTRANCE TRANSITION and its four successive names, the dialogue with Gita on the Indian inn.

- "If you can't draw a diagram of it, it isn't a pattern." (p. 267)

**15. The reality of patterns** (pp. 277–304). Summary: "*We may then gradually improve these patterns which we share, by testing them against experience: we can determine, very simply, whether these patterns make our surroundings live, or not, by recognizing how they make us feel.*" (p. 277). A pattern can be well formed and still dead, because analysis never guarantees we have all the forces. Feeling responds to the whole, so feeling is the test, and feeling, unlike opinion or taste, shows near-total agreement. Yet "feelings themselves are not the essence of the matter" (p. 297), and a real pattern does not judge which forces "ought" to exist. Examples: the "madhouse balcony", Le Corbusier's radiant city, the 10,000-bed São Paulo hospital, the Peru houses.

- "in the domain of patterns, where people seem to agree 90, 95, even 99 percent of the time, we may treat this agreement as an extraordinary, almost shattering, discovery, about the solidity of human feelings, and we may certainly use it as scientific." (p. 294)

**16. The structure of a language** (pp. 305–324). Summary: "*Once we have understood how to discover individual patterns which are alive, we may then make a language for ourselves, for any building task we face. The structure of the language is created by the network of connections among individual patterns: and the language lives, or not, as a totality, to the degree these patterns form a whole.*" (p. 305). Each pattern depends on the larger patterns it helps complete and the smaller ones that complete it; the links are as much the language as the patterns. A good language is morphologically complete (no gaps in the "species" it generates) and functionally complete (it generates only forces it resolves). Making the language is the real design work. Example: a language for a garden.

- "So, the real work of any process of design lies in this task of making up the language, from which you can later generate the one particular design." (p. 324)

**17. The evolution of a common language for a town** (pp. 325–348). Summary: "*Then finally, from separate languages for different building tasks, we can create a larger structure still, a structure of structures, evolving constantly, which is the common language for a town. This is the gate.*" (p. 325). Languages overlap and unify; *A Pattern Language* is one attempt, and all 253 names are listed (pp. 331–336). But a published language is not yet alive: it must be local, personal and re-created in each mind, as a child re-invents grammar. A common language is a "pattern pool" like a gene pool; because patterns change one at a time it evolves cumulatively, and "No language is ever finished" (p. 346). Example: three local patterns generalised into COMMON AREA AT THE HEART.

- "A living language must constantly be re-created in each person's mind." (p. 338)

### Part: The way

Divider: "Once we have built the gate, we can pass through it to the practice of the timeless way." (p. 349)

**18. The genetic power of language** (pp. 351–364). Summary: "*Now we shall begin to see in detail how the rich and complex order of a town can grow from thousands of creative acts. For once we have a common pattern language in our town, we shall all have the power to make our streets and buildings live, through our most ordinary acts. The language, like a seed, is the genetic system which gives our millions of small acts the power to form a whole.*" (p. 351). "The expertise is in the language" (p. 353). A town, like an organism, is a flux in which construction and repair are one process, and the language is the invariant behind the flux. Because patterns are linked, each small act also helps the larger patterns around it.

- "Each process of construction helps repair some larger whole, of which it is merely a part. No thing is whole unto itself." (p. 361)

**19. Differentiating space** (pp. 365–384). Summary: "*Within this process, every individual act of building is a process in which space gets differentiated. It is not a process of addition, in which pre-formed parts are combined to create a whole: but a process of unfolding, like the evolution of an embryo, in which the whole precedes its parts, and actually gives birth to them, by splitting.*" (p. 365). Nothing with the character of nature can be assembled from preformed parts; design is a sequence of differentiations of a whole. It only works in the right order, larger before smaller, under three stated conditions (p. 379), so the sequence "is therefore the key to that design" (p. 382). Examples: the embryo, patterns as "clouds", his balcony built around a pine tree.

- "Each pattern is an operator which differentiates space: that is, it creates distinctions where no distinction was before." (p. 373)

**20. One pattern at a time** (pp. 385–402). Summary: "*The process of unfolding goes step by step, one pattern at a time. Each step brings just one pattern to life: and the intensity of the result depends on the intensity of each one of these individual steps.*" (p. 385). To place a pattern, recall the best instances you know, close your eyes, and ask what the most wonderful version would be here. No compromise between patterns is needed, since each is a rule of transformation. Examples: beans strung across a hut window, the Topkapi room, the imagined orange in Lima.

- "each pattern has the power to transform any configuration by injecting a new configuration into it, without essentially disturbing any essentials of the configuration which was there before." (p. 400)

**21. Shaping one building** (pp. 403–426). Summary: "*From a sequence of these individual patterns, whole buildings with the character of nature will form themselves within your thoughts, as easily as sentences.*" (p. 403). Worked case: a week of notes designing a 400-square-foot cottage behind the office from a 38-pattern language, starting with repair of the site, eyes closed, staked out on the ground, with no drawing, because only the mind is fluid enough. He admits the result is "still immensely far" from the houses in the opening photographs (p. 425).

- "Each new pattern in the sequence transforms the whole design created by the previous patterns—it transforms it as a whole, it shakes it up, and realigns it." (p. 422)

**22. Shaping a group of buildings** (pp. 427–454). Summary: "*In the same way, groups of people can conceive their larger public buildings, on the ground, by following a common pattern language, almost as if they had a single mind.*" (p. 427). Worked case: a psychiatric clinic in rural California laid out in a week on site by its director, staff and two CES members. The group agrees a language of about forty patterns, then fixes one pattern at a time with chalk and stones; the site replaces the single mind. The building was then detailed conventionally and "almost destroyed" (p. 451).

- "The site speaks to the people—the building forms itself—and people experience it as something received, not created." (p. 449)

**23. The process of construction** (pp. 455–474). Summary: "*Once the buildings are conceived like this, they can be built, directly, from a few simple marks made in the ground—again within a common language, but directly, and without the use of drawings.*" (p. 455). Construction must continue the differentiating sequence. Modular parts and working drawings both force identical parts; the builder should use rough drawings and standard *processes* that yield unique parts, as a spider's one process yields unique webs. He gives a full sequence from stakes to basket vaults and carved ornament (pp. 464–472).

- "Each process (given by a pattern) takes the configuration which has been produced by the previous processes, and adapts itself to them." (p. 474)

**24. The process of repair** (pp. 475–492). Summary: "*Next, several acts of building, each one done to repair and magnify the product of the previous acts, will slowly generate a larger and more complex whole than any single act can generate.*" (p. 475). Design is prediction and always partly wrong, so each new act should start from what is wrong now and mend it; repair in this sense transforms the whole. Left-over space must become as whole as what flanks it. Piecemeal growth is a second process, complementary to differentiation and more powerful because it corrects mistakes. Examples: the fifth laboratory, the slow growth of twelve houses.

- "Slowly, at every level, the arrangement of wholes becomes so dense that there are no gaps between the wholes: every part, and every part between two parts, is whole." (p. 490)

**25. The slow emergence of a town** (pp. 493–510). Summary: "*Finally, within the framework of a common language, millions of individual acts of building will together generate a town which is alive, and whole, and unpredictable, without control—this is the slow emergence of the quality without a name, as if from nothing.*" (p. 493). Large patterns emerge from many small transformations, steered by shared awareness of the larger pattern and by incentives, with no master plan. This needs a nested hierarchy of groups (person, family, cluster, neighbourhood, community, town), each owning its common land and helping the next level up. The outcome is certain in kind, unpredictable in detail. Example: CITY COUNTRY FINGERS grown by incentives.

- "the postulate that it is possible for all the large-scale order of a town to be created purely by means of incremental piecemeal acts." (p. 496)

**26. Its ageless character** (pp. 511–528). Summary: "*And as the whole emerges, we shall see it take that ageless character which gives the timeless way its name. This character is a specific, morphological character, sharp and precise, which must come into being any time a building or a town becomes alive: it is the physical embodiment, in buildings, of the quality without a name.*" (p. 511). Buildings made this way resemble those of all traditional cultures, because that is the morphology of things fitted to their forces. Its marks: greater differentiation, edges that are places with thickness, a balance of order and disorder, parts that interlock so none can be "lifted out". Modern prismatic geometry is "the naive order" (p. 526). Example: the prefabricated window versus the window that is one with its wall.

- "There are no gaps between the parts, because each gap is just as much a part itself." (p. 523)

### Part: The kernel of the way

Divider: "And yet the timeless way is not complete, and will not fully generate the quality without a name, until we leave the gate behind." (p. 529)

**27. The kernel of the way** (pp. 531–549). Summary: "*Indeed this ageless character has nothing, in the end, to do with languages. The language, and the processes which stem from it, merely release the fundamental order which is native to us. They do not teach us, they only remind us of what we know already, and of what we shall discover time and time again, when we give up our ideas and opinions, and do exactly what emerges from ourselves.*" (p. 531). A building lives only when its maker is egoless, working without an "outward glance" from a void with no image; even Wright and Aalto fail this. Patterns used mechanically are just more images: a place can have the right patterns and be dead, and "you can only make a building live when you are free enough to reject even the very patterns which are helping you" (p. 542). Yet the language is how one becomes egoless, by restoring trust in what one already feels. Examples: the fruit stand, the Danish fishing boat, the strawberries.

- "It is the gate which leads you to the state of mind, in which you live so close to your own heart that you no longer need a language." (p. 547)

## 5. Links to the other books

Verified against the source texts where a page is given; otherwise from general knowledge and marked "unverified".

- **Quality without a name → life, wholeness, and finally "I".** NoO Book 1 opens by pointing back: "I have described this very ordinary but intensely living quality of buildings and places in the first few chapters of THE TIMELESS WAY OF BUILDING" (Book 1, p. 36; also p. 62 n. 5). There the quality stops being unnameable: it becomes degrees of life, defined structurally as wholeness and a field of centers. Book 4 then reads it metaphysically: "this wonderful lazy substance — the quality without a name — as spirit made actual" (Book 4, p. 302). Timeless Way already allows that the religious hint "is accurate" (p. 39).
- **Patterns → centers.** NoO renames and absorbs them: "The entities we called patterns were — albeit in an early formulation — somewhat similar to the entities I now call centers … every pattern … was, in effect, a rule for making or partly making some important type of center" (Book 2, p. 344). Timeless Way is already close to the recursive definition of a center (elements dissolving into relationships, p. 89; a pattern as "a field", p. 223; dependence on larger and smaller patterns, p. 312).
- **Forces.** "Resolution of forces" is the explanatory core here, inherited from *Notes on the Synthesis of Form* (unverified); NoO largely replaces it with geometric relations among centers.
- **Ageless character → the fifteen properties.** Book 1 says the properties "may be thought of as an elaboration of the observations, recorded more informally in THE TIMELESS WAY OF BUILDING … chapter 23. It was the content of that chapter, written in 1975, which stirred in me the need to start the observations that are recorded here" (Book 1, p. 242 n. 1). In this edition the matching content is chapter 26 (thick edges, differentiation, order and disorder, parts that cannot be lifted out, no gaps: compare boundaries, gradients, roughness, deep interlock, positive space); chapter 23 is construction. "Chapter 23" is probably a draft numbering; check before citing.
- **Differentiation and repair → structure-preserving transformations and unfolding.** Ch. 19's whole-before-parts and ch. 20's pattern that transforms "without essentially disturbing any essentials" (p. 400), with ch. 24's "wholeness which is specific to that moment" (p. 486), are the direct ancestors of Book 2's unfolding wholeness. Book 2 returns to Timeless Way "more deeply" and reuses the Samoan canoe (Book 2, p. 86).
- **Language → sequences and form languages.** The claim that sequence is the key (p. 382) becomes Book 2's chapter on the sequence of unfolding, and construction patterns become "form language". The later term "generative codes" is post-NoO usage (unverified).
- **Feeling as test → mirror of the self.** Ch. 15's feeling-versus-opinion distinction and its claim of near-universal agreement become the explicit tests of Book 1 ch. 8–9.
- **The gate and the kernel.** NoO keeps the kernel (egolessness, starting from a void) and makes it central in Book 4, but drops the gate metaphor: patterns are no longer the necessary route, and the maker works on wholeness directly. This is the main supersession.
- **A Pattern Language (vol. 2)** supplies the 253 patterns that ch. 14–17 explain and list. Timeless Way is franker about their status: each is "still only a guess" (p. 269) and the published language "not yet fully living" (p. 336).
- **The Oregon Experiment (vol. 3)** applies ch. 24–25 (piecemeal growth, repair) to a campus; ch. 17 (p. 330) cites the Oregon pattern DEPARTMENT HEARTH. **The Production of Houses** later tests ch. 23's construction process at Mexicali (unverified).

## 7. Text quality notes

- **Systematic OCR substitutions, worst in italics** (so in exactly the summaries and headlines that matter): "how" as hore/horo, "now" as nore/nor, "town" as toren/toron/loren/corn, "law" as lare/laro, "few" as fero, "view" as vier, "own" as oron, "slow" as shore/show, "too"/"to" as 100/10/1o, and t read as l or i (pallern, limeless, qualizy). All quotes above are corrected silently; check any new quote against the searchable PDF. The detailed table of contents (pp. ix–xv) gives a second, equally noisy copy of every chapter summary.
- **Italic headlines are broken and displaced.** Many are split mid-sentence, with their last words dropped out of italics or moved to a later line (e.g. pp. 84, 150, 313); some body phrases are wrongly italicised (pp. 86–87). Italic spans are approximate.
- **Dropped or duplicated words:** p. 298 "cars are where the action" (lacks "is"); p. 50 "Another traveling with the gypsies"; p. 206 a repeated sentence fragment. p. 160, the "made/generated" passage ends in an ellipsis in the source.
- **Scrambled passages, not quotable without the PDF:** the Gita dialogue (pp. 270–274, speaker turns out of order); the context–forces–configuration example (p. 253); the morphological-law formula (p. 90); barn dimensions (p. 179, "3* feet", a missing lower bound for column spacing); the photographer credits (p. 552).
- **Diagrams lost:** the pattern network drawings (pp. 313–314, rendered as garbled pattern names) and embryo drawings (pp. 370–371) have no figure files. Captions for the ch. 6 diagrams sit out of order beside their images.
- **Pattern names:** upper-case names survive well but some are hyphen-split or interleaved (pp. 331–336, e.g. NINE PER CENT PARKING). "Volume 2" appears as "volume a" (p. 248) and "volume z" (p. 310).
