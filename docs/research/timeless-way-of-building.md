## 2. The argument, chapter by chapter

The book has five parts, each opened by a one-sentence divider: **The timeless way** (ch. 1), **The quality** (ch. 2–8), **The gate** (ch. 9–17), **The way** (ch. 18–26), **The kernel of the way** (ch. 27). Each chapter opens with an italic summary, given here as "Summary".

### Part: The timeless way

Divider: "A building or a town will only be alive to the extent that it is governed by the timeless way." (p. 1)

**1. The timeless way** (pp. 3–16). Summary: "*It is a process which brings order out of nothing but ourselves; it cannot be attained, but it will happen of its own accord, if we will only let it.*" (p. 3). All building that lives, from villages to the Alhambra, comes from one invariant process, now identifiable through two ideas: patterns (the constituents of buildings) and pattern languages (the generative processes that produce them). The process is precise but not mechanical: it frees a capacity people already have and are afraid to use. The chapter previews the whole arc: learn a discipline, then drop it.

- "There is one timeless way of building. It is thousands of years old, and the same today as it has always been." (p. 7)
- "Indeed it turns out, in the end, that what this method does is simply free us from all method." (p. 13)
- "This is the timeless way of building: learning the discipline—and shedding it." (p. 16)

### Part: The quality

Divider: "To seek the timeless way we must first know the quality without a name." (p. 17)

**2. The quality without a name** (pp. 19–40). Summary: "*There is a central quality which is the root criterion of life and spirit in a man, a town, a building, or a wilderness. This quality is objective and precise, but it cannot be named.*" (p. 19). The difference between good and bad buildings is objective: it is freedom from inner contradiction, a system being "at one with itself". He circles the quality with seven words, each of which fits and then fails: alive, whole, comfortable, free, exact, egoless, eternal. Examples: the peach tree against the garden wall (p. 25), the well-made fire (pp. 29–30), windblown trees versus the eroding gully (p. 31), the pot of tea and cushions (pp. 32–33), the bird table (p. 35), the carved bench (p. 36), the Japanese fishpond (p. 38).

- "It is a subtle kind of freedom from inner contradictions." (p. 26)
- "The fact that this quality cannot be named does not mean that it is vague or imprecise. It is impossible to name because it is unerringly precise. Words fail to capture it because it is much more precise than any word." (p. 29)
- "It is a slightly bitter quality." (p. 40)

**3. Being alive** (pp. 41–54). Summary: "*The search which we make for this quality, in our own lives, is the central search of any person, and the crux of any individual person's story. It is the search for those moments and situations when we are most alive.*" (p. 41). We know the quality first in ourselves: it appears when we let go of images of how to live and our inner forces are resolved. Because we know the feeling, we can recognise it in places, by asking "whether they are like us when we are free" (p. 53). Person and place create the quality in each other. Examples: Kurosawa's *Ikiru* (p. 49), the high-wire artist (pp. 49–50), the gypsies in the rain (pp. 50–51).

- "Places which have this quality, invite this quality to come to life in us. And when we have this quality in us, we tend to make it come to life in towns and buildings which we help to build. It is a self-supporting, self-maintaining, generating quality." (pp. 53–54)

**4. Patterns of events** (pp. 55–74). Summary: "*In order to define this quality in buildings and in towns, we must begin by understanding that every place is given its character by certain patterns of events that keep on happening there.*" (p. 55). A place is what keeps happening in it, human and non-human; a life is governed by perhaps a dozen recurring patterns of events. These events cannot be separated from the space where they occur: culture names them by spatial elements (porch, barbershop, sidewalk), and the Bombay sidewalk and the New York sidewalk are two different patterns, not one pattern with two uses. Examples: anticuchos in Lima and chestnuts in Geneva (p. 63), the reflecting pool versus the stream with a rowing boat (p. 65), his own daily round (p. 67), "watching the world go by" on a porch (p. 70).

- "The action and the space are indivisible. The action is supported by this kind of space. The space supports this kind of action. The two form a unit, a pattern of events in space." (p. 70)
- "The life which happens in a building or a town is not merely anchored in the space but made up from the space itself." (p. 74)

**5. Patterns of space** (pp. 75–100). Summary: "*These patterns of events are always interlocked with certain geometric patterns in the space. Indeed, as we shall see, each building and each town is ultimately made out of these patterns in the space, and out of nothing else: they are the atoms and the molecules from which a building or a town is made.*" (p. 75). The apparent "elements" of a building (nave, aisle, door) vary every time, so they are not what repeats. What repeats is relationships, and on inspection the elements themselves dissolve into patterns of relationships among smaller patterns. Each pattern is a morphological law of the form X → r(A, B, …): "Within a context of type X, the parts A, B, ... are related by the relationship r" (p. 90). A building is defined by a few dozen patterns, a city by a few hundred. Examples: the gothic cathedral (p. 86), the American metropolitan region (pp. 86–87), freeway–cloverleaf–lane (pp. 89–90), the door (p. 91), the porch (pp. 92–93), Venice and London (pp. 96–98).

- "And finally, the things which seem like elements dissolve, and leave a fabric of relationships behind, which is the stuff that actually repeats itself, and gives the structure to a building or a town." (p. 89)
- "For the pattern in the space is, precisely, the precondition, the requirement, which allows the pattern of events to happen." (p. 92)

**6. Patterns which are alive** (pp. 101–122). Summary: "*The specific patterns out of which a building or a town is made may be alive or dead. To the extent they are alive, they let our inner forces loose, and set us free; but when they are dead they keep us locked in inner conflict.*" (p. 101). A living pattern lets the forces in a situation resolve themselves; a dead one sets up conflicts no one can resolve, leaving people in chronic stress. He then refuses the anthropocentric reading: patterns are good not because they serve us but because they are stable and self-sustaining in their own right, like ripples in sand. Three paired examples carry it: work mixed with family versus work separated (pp. 107–108), the living versus the dead courtyard (pp. 109–110, 121), the window place versus "holes in the wall" (pp. 111–112). Also: sand ripples (p. 117), the orchard corner (p. 118), cancer as a limit case of stability (p. 118), the Greek whitewashed band versus the indoor Los Angeles café held in place by law (pp. 119–120).

- "In short, a pattern lives when it allows its own internal forces to resolve themselves." (p. 120)
- "Good patterns are good because to some extent each one of them reaches the quality without a name itself." (p. 116)

**7. The multiplicity of living patterns** (pp. 123–136). Summary: "*The more living patterns there are in a thing—a room, a building, or a town—the more it comes to life as an entirety, the more it glows, the more it has this self-maintaining fire, which is the quality without a name.*" (p. 123). Life is a property of systems of patterns, not single ones. A dead pattern cannot contain its forces, which leak out and destabilise neighbouring patterns; a living pattern needs both the larger patterns it sits in and the smaller ones it is made of. Examples: the unbraced column–beam joint that cracks and lets in water (pp. 127–128), the dead courtyard pushing children into the street (pp. 128–129), WINDOW PLACE needing LOW WINDOWSILL, CASEMENT WINDOW and SMALL PANES (pp. 131–132), the whole entrance (pp. 132–133), the whole neighbourhood (pp. 133–134).

- "And finally the quality without a name appears, not when an isolated pattern lives, but when an entire system of patterns, interdependent at many levels, is all stable and alive." (p. 135)

**8. The quality itself** (pp. 137–154). Summary: "*And when a building has this fire, then it becomes a part of nature. Like ocean waves, or blades of grass, its parts are governed by the endless play of repetition and variety, created in the presence of the fact that all things pass. This is the quality itself.*" (p. 137). A place made of living patterns has a specific geometric signature, "the character of nature": the same patterns repeat everywhere, yet every part is unique because each pattern meets different local conditions. This is the opposite of modular construction. The geometry is therefore loose and slightly irregular, and it cannot be had without accepting that the thing will age and die. Examples: oak trees, waves and drops (pp. 144–145), atoms in a crystal, each different by position (pp. 145–146), SUNNY PLACE turning a corner (p. 151), soft tile and fruit crushed on the path (p. 153).

- "The repetition of patterns is quite a different thing from the repetition of parts." (p. 150)
- "Indeed, the different parts will be unique because the patterns are the same." (p. 151)
- "The character of nature can't arise without the presence and the consciousness of death." (p. 153)

### Part: The gate

Divider: "To reach the quality without a name we must then build a living pattern language as a gate." (p. 155)

**9. The flower and the seed** (pp. 157–166). Summary: "*This quality in buildings and in towns cannot be made, but only generated, indirectly, by the ordinary actions of the people, just as a flower cannot be made, but only generated from the seed.*" (p. 157). Organic complexity cannot be designed from above; it can only be generated by a process in which each part adapts autonomously to local conditions. Autonomy alone gives chaos, so the parts need a shared code, as cells share a genetic code. Hence the question that opens the Gate: is there such a code for building? Examples: the Samoan canoe (p. 159), the brush stroke (p. 160), the flower built with tweezers (p. 162).

- "When a thing is made, it has the will of the maker in it. But when it is generated, it is generated, freely, by the operation of egoless rules, acting on the reality of the situation, and giving birth, of their own accord" (p. 160)
- "the great complexity of an organic system, which is essential to its life, cannot be created from above directly; it can only be generated indirectly." (p. 162)

**10. Our pattern languages** (pp. 167–192). Summary: "*The people can shape buildings for themselves, and have done it for centuries, by using languages which I call pattern languages. A pattern language gives each person who uses it, the power to create an infinite variety of new and unique buildings, just as his ordinary language gives him the power to create an infinite variety of sentences.*" (p. 167). Neither function nor copying explains why traditional barns are both alike and endlessly varied; the builder carries a system of patterns in his mind. Patterns in the mind differ from patterns in the world: they are generative rules with an imperative force. A pattern language is a finite combinatory system, like a natural language, in which patterns are both the elements and the rules. Examples: California barn rules of thumb (pp. 179–181), hillside terracing (pp. 182–183), the Bernese Oberland farmhouse language (p. 187), the stone houses of southern Italy and their streets (pp. 188–191).

- "Each pattern is a rule which describes what you have to do to generate the entity which it defines." (p. 182)
- "both ordinary languages and pattern languages are finite combinatory systems which allow us to create an infinite variety of unique combinations, appropriate to different circumstances, at will." (p. 187)

**11. Our pattern languages (continued)** (pp. 193–210). Summary: "*These pattern languages are not confined to villages and farm society. All acts of building are governed by a pattern language of some sort, and the patterns in the world are there, entirely because they are created by the languages which people use.*" (p. 193). Modern freeways, gas stations and office towers also come from languages: handbooks, codes, bank lending rules, magazine ideas. Architects make perhaps 5 percent of buildings (p. 199); the rest come from thousands of people following rules of thumb. Everyone, Palladio and Wright included, designs from the language in their mind, and language enables creativity as English grammar enables speech. Examples: Stevenage and Ebenezer Howard, the AASHO manual, the Park Avenue zoning envelope, the kitchen-window shelves (pp. 200–201), "lay out a simple cottage" (p. 204).

- "Your pattern language is the sum total of your knowledge of how to build." (pp. 202–203)
- "A pattern language is really nothing more than a precise way of describing someone's experience of building." (p. 207)

**12. The creative power of language** (pp. 211–224). Summary: "*And, beyond that, it is not just the shape of towns and buildings which comes to them from pattern languages, it is their quality as well. Even the life and beauty of the most awe-inspiring great religious buildings came from the languages their builders used.*" (p. 211). Chartres was not designed at a drawing board; it was built by people steeped in one shared language. The deepest knowledge is ordinary and can be stated as simple rules; greatness lies in observing them accurately and refusing to let any go. Patterns are fluid fields, not fixed components, which is why combining them can yield art. Examples: cathedral patterns (pp. 215–216), the hairline of a third colour between two colours in old Turkish prayer rugs (pp. 219–220), light on two sides of every room (p. 220), the Ise shrine (p. 221).

- "the most mystical, most religious, most wonderful—these are not less ordinary than most things—they are more ordinary than most things." (p. 219)
- "Each pattern is a field—not fixed, but a bundle of relationships, capable of being different each time that it occurs, yet deep enough to bestow life wherever it occurs." (p. 223)

**13. The breakdown of language** (pp. 225–242). Summary: "*But in our time the languages have broken down. Since they are no longer shared, the processes which keep them deep have broken down: and it is therefore virtually impossible for anybody, in our time, to make a building live.*" (p. 225). Dead places are made from languages too. A living language is widely shared, covers the whole of life, and keeps users directly connected to building, so its patterns are constantly tested. In industrial society languages became specialised, private and fragmented; people lost confidence in their own feelings, and control from above (urban design, system building, planning law) made matters worse. Building is a genetic process, so the real task of architecture is the language, not the individual design. Examples: his own university office and its nine-pattern language (p. 228), Japanese children learning to lay out a house (p. 230), the seminar rooms of Berkeley's College of Environmental Design (p. 234), Le Corbusier's Marseilles block and light on one side (p. 235), the fireplace designed for others (p. 236).

- "The time when a pattern language was a song, in which people could sing the whole of life, is gone. The pattern languages in society are dead. They are ashes and fragments in the people's hands." (p. 237)
- "the central task of \"architecture\" is the creation of a single, shared, evolving, pattern language, which everyone contributes to, and everyone can use." (p. 241)

**14. Patterns which can be shared** (pp. 243–276). Summary: "*To work our way toward a shared and living language once again, we must first learn how to discover patterns which are deep, and capable of generating life.*" (p. 243). When languages are no longer implicit they must be made explicit. This is the book's formal definition of a pattern: a three-part rule, Context → System of forces → Configuration (p. 253), making two empirical claims that can be true or false. Patterns are discovered by observation (sorting places that feel good from those that do not and finding the invariant), from negative examples, or by abstract argument. The heart of a pattern is a "morphological feeling", not a formula, yet to be usable it must be treated as a thing, drawn and named. Examples: the alcoves of Ostenfeldgaarden (pp. 249–253), ENTRANCE TRANSITION and its successive names (pp. 254–257, 263–268), SOUTH FACING OUTDOORS (p. 258), PARALLEL STREETS found by reasoning (pp. 259–260), the "rough circle" (p. 263), the Peru houses (p. 264), the dialogue with Gita about the Indian pilgrims' inn (pp. 270–274).

- "Each pattern is a three-part rule, which expresses a relation between a certain context, a problem, and a solution." (p. 247)
- "The pattern is, in short, at the same time a thing, which happens in the world, and the rule which tells us how to create that thing, and when we must create it." (p. 247)
- "If you can't draw a diagram of it, it isn't a pattern." (p. 267)

**15. The reality of patterns** (pp. 277–304). Summary: "*We may then gradually improve these patterns which we share, by testing them against experience: we can determine, very simply, whether these patterns make our surroundings live, or not, by recognizing how they make us feel.*" (p. 277). A pattern can be well formed and its statements true, yet be dead, because we can never be sure analytically that we have all the forces. Feeling is the reliable test, since feeling responds to the whole system; and feeling (as distinct from opinion, taste or ideas) shows 90–99 percent agreement among people of one culture, which he treats as a scientific finding. True feeling takes effort to reach. Finally, feeling is the indicator, not the essence: what matters is the quality itself, and a real pattern makes no judgement about which forces "ought" to exist. Examples: the "madhouse balcony" (p. 283), Le Corbusier's radiant city and territoriality (pp. 284–285), ALCOVE, T-JUNCTIONS, MOSAIC OF SUBCULTURES (pp. 287–288), modular aluminium panels (p. 290), the San Francisco meeting and POOLS AND STREAMS (pp. 292–293), the 10,000-bed São Paulo hospital (p. 293), the fountain flowing four ways (p. 296), NETWORK OF PATHS AND CARS (pp. 298–299), the *sala* and front-door niches in the Peru houses (pp. 299–300), the street mask (p. 301).

- "To do this, we must rely on feelings more than intellect." (p. 286)
- "in the domain of patterns, where people seem to agree 90, 95, even 99 percent of the time, we may treat this agreement as an extraordinary, almost shattering, discovery, about the solidity of human feelings, and we may certainly use it as scientific." (p. 294)
- "But even so, feelings themselves are not the essence of the matter." (p. 297)

**16. The structure of a language** (pp. 305–324). Summary: "*Once we have understood how to discover individual patterns which are alive, we may then make a language for ourselves, for any building task we face. The structure of the language is created by the network of connections among individual patterns: and the language lives, or not, as a totality, to the degree these patterns form a whole.*" (p. 305). Patterns are not isolated: each depends on the larger patterns it helps complete and the smaller ones that complete it, and these links are as much the language as the patterns. A language is good when it is morphologically complete (you can visualise the "species" of thing it generates, with no gaps) and functionally complete (it generates only forces it resolves), with each pattern's principal components, a handful, directly beneath it. The real design work is making the language; using it takes hours. Examples: a language for a garden (pp. 309–314), the garage (p. 311), PRIVATE TERRACE ON THE STREET under two different parents (pp. 314–315), the blank-wall gap under BUILDING EDGE (p. 319), the five components of HALF-HIDDEN GARDEN (pp. 320–322).

- "Each pattern then, depends both on the smaller patterns it contains, and on the larger patterns within which it is contained." (p. 312)
- "So, the real work of any process of design lies in this task of making up the language, from which you can later generate the one particular design." (p. 324)

**17. The evolution of a common language for a town** (pp. 325–348). Summary: "*Then finally, from separate languages for different building tasks, we can create a larger structure still, a structure of structures, evolving constantly, which is the common language for a town. This is the gate.*" (p. 325). Separate languages overlap and can be unified; *A Pattern Language* is one such attempt, and the chapter lists all 253 pattern names by scale (pp. 331–336). But a published language is not yet living: it must be local, personal, and re-created in each mind as a child re-invents grammar. A common language is a "pattern pool" analogous to a gene pool, and because patterns can be changed one at a time it evolves cumulatively and never finishes. Examples: DEPARTMENT HEARTH, TANGENT PATHS and FAMILY ROOM CIRCULATION generalised into COMMON AREA AT THE HEART (p. 330), Peruvian versus English INTIMACY GRADIENT (p. 340).

- "A living language must constantly be re-created in each person's mind." (p. 338)
- "The common pattern language is not any one language which any one person has in his mind—it is defined by the overall distribution of patterns in the pattern pool." (p. 343)
- "there is no static perfect language, which, once defined, will stay defined forever. No language is ever finished." (p. 346)

### Part: The way

Divider: "Once we have built the gate, we can pass through it to the practice of the timeless way." (p. 349)

**18. The genetic power of language** (pp. 351–364). Summary: "*Now we shall begin to see in detail how the rich and complex order of a town can grow from thousands of creative acts. For once we have a common pattern language in our town, we shall all have the power to make our streets and buildings live, through our most ordinary acts. The language, like a seed, is the genetic system which gives our millions of small acts the power to form a whole.*" (p. 351). With a shared language "the expertise is in the language" (p. 353), and people must shape their own surroundings because patterns are patterns of action as well as space. A town, like an organism, is a flux in which construction and repair are one process; the language is the invariant behind the flux. Because every pattern is linked to larger and smaller ones, each small act also helps build the larger patterns around it. Examples: sub-languages for a window seat, a house, common land, a city boundary (pp. 359–360), PRIVATE TERRACE ON THE STREET helping form GREEN STREETS (p. 362).

- "Each process of construction helps repair some larger whole, of which it is merely a part. No thing is whole unto itself." (p. 361)
- "It is not the end product of this process which is alive, but the incessant flux itself." (p. 364)

**19. Differentiating space** (pp. 365–384). Summary: "*Within this process, every individual act of building is a process in which space gets differentiated. It is not a process of addition, in which pre-formed parts are combined to create a whole: but a process of unfolding, like the evolution of an embryo, in which the whole precedes its parts, and actually gives birth to them, by splitting.*" (p. 365). Nothing with the character of nature can be assembled from preformed modular parts, because each part must be shaped by its position in the whole. Design is therefore a sequence of differentiations of a whole, as in an embryo, and each pattern is an operator that draws a new distinction. This only works if patterns are taken in the right order, larger before smaller, with three stated conditions on sequence (p. 379); the sequence "is therefore the key to that design" (p. 382). Examples: the embryo (pp. 370–371), patterns as vague "clouds" made precise by smaller patterns (pp. 372–373), his own balcony built around a pine tree, columns at 5½–3–5½ (pp. 374–377), patterns for a house read in random order (pp. 377–378).

- "In the process of differentiation, the whole gives birth to its parts: the parts appear as folds in a cloth of three dimensional space which is gradually crinkled." (p. 370)
- "Each pattern is an operator which differentiates space: that is, it creates distinctions where no distinction was before." (p. 373)

**20. One pattern at a time** (pp. 385–402). Summary: "*The process of unfolding goes step by step, one pattern at a time. Each step brings just one pattern to life: and the intensity of the result depends on the intensity of each one of these individual steps.*" (p. 385). To inject a pattern, recall the best instances you know, close your eyes, and ask what the most wonderful version would be in this place; do not design it consciously. Taken seriously, each pattern produces an "almost startling character". There is no need to compromise between patterns or worry about later ones, because each pattern is a rule of transformation and the order of the language removes most conflicts. Examples: the beach-house stair landing that was not an ENTRANCE TRANSITION (pp. 392–393), beans strung across a hut window for FILTERED LIGHT (p. 394), the Topkapi room (p. 395), finding the Peru road by biting an imagined orange (pp. 397–398).

- "Your mind is a medium within which the creative spark that jumps between the pattern and the world can happen. You yourself are only the medium for this creative spark, not its originator." (p. 397)
- "each pattern has the power to transform any configuration by injecting a new configuration into it, without essentially disturbing any essentials of the configuration which was there before." (p. 400)

**21. Shaping one building** (pp. 403–426). Summary: "*From a sequence of these individual patterns, whole buildings with the character of nature will form themselves within your thoughts, as easily as sentences.*" (p. 403). A worked case: his week of notes designing a 400-square-foot cottage/workshop behind the office from a 38-pattern language, beginning with repair of the site and proceeding pattern by pattern, eyes closed, staking out on the ground. No drawing was made: only the mind is fluid enough for each new pattern to reshape the whole. Learning the language is slow; using it is fast, like speech. He admits the result is "still immensely far" from the houses in the chapter's opening photographs (p. 425). Examples: the cottage sequence (pp. 407–421), the stile through Susie's window (pp. 415–416), sentences on tracing paper (p. 423).

- "Each new pattern in the sequence transforms the whole design created by the previous patterns—it transforms it as a whole, it shakes it up, and realigns it." (p. 422)
- "You are only the medium in which the patterns come to life, and of their own accord give birth to something new." (p. 426)

**22. Shaping a group of buildings** (pp. 427–454). Summary: "*In the same way, groups of people can conceive their larger public buildings, on the ground, by following a common pattern language, almost as if they had a single mind.*" (p. 427). A second worked case: a 25,000-square-foot psychiatric clinic in rural California, designed in a week on the site by its director Dr. Ryan, his staff and two CES members. The group first settles a language of about forty patterns (the medium for resolving disagreements), then walks the site fixing one pattern at a time with chalk and stones; the site replaces the single mind as the fluid medium. Positions chosen independently fall within ten feet of each other (p. 438). The cautionary ending matters: the building was then detailed conventionally on the drawing board and "almost destroyed" (p. 451), which motivates chapter 23.

- "The site speaks to the people—the building forms itself—and people experience it as something received, not created." (p. 449)
- "a building laid out by a pattern language process, and which comes to life because of it, will die again, quite certainly, when it is built, unless the process of construction is the same" (p. 452)

**23. The process of construction** (pp. 455–474). Summary: "*Once the buildings are conceived like this, they can be built, directly, from a few simple marks made in the ground—again within a common language, but directly, and without the use of drawings.*" (p. 455). Construction must continue the same differentiating sequence, with patterns now operating on the building itself. Modular components and working drawings both force identical parts and so kill the detail; the builder should work from rough drawings and standard *processes* that yield unique parts, as a spider's standard process yields unique webs. He gives a concrete sequence: stake out, corner columns, stiffening columns, perimeter beams, door and window frames, woven-basket vaults, walls, floors, terraces, carved ornament (pp. 464–472). Examples: four-foot panels "tyrannize the geometry of the room" (p. 461), the model of a four-storey apartment building for twenty-seven families who each designed their own apartment (p. 473).

- "The individual processes are standardized, and very simple. But the actual parts which are produced are infinitely various" (p. 463)
- "Each process (given by a pattern) takes the configuration which has been produced by the previous processes, and adapts itself to them." (p. 474)

**24. The process of repair** (pp. 475–492). Summary: "*Next, several acts of building, each one done to repair and magnify the product of the previous acts, will slowly generate a larger and more complex whole than any single act can generate.*" (p. 475). No building is right first time, because design is prediction. So every later act should start from what is wrong with the present state and use the new work to mend it; repair in this sense transforms the whole rather than restoring it. Gaps and left-over places must be made as whole as the parts either side. Piecemeal growth is thus a second process, complementary to differentiation and more powerful, because it corrects mistakes. Examples: the missing garden wall and terrace mended by one act (pp. 480–481), the fifth laboratory (pp. 481–482), the air between the leaves (pp. 482–483), the Zen master's "when I eat, I eat" (p. 484), the slow growth of twelve houses (pp. 487–491).

- "every entity is changing constantly: and that at every moment we use the defects of the present state as the starting point for the definition of the new state." (p. 485)
- "Slowly, at every level, the arrangement of wholes becomes so dense that there are no gaps between the wholes: every part, and every part between two parts, is whole." (p. 490)

**25. The slow emergence of a town** (pp. 493–510). Summary: "*Finally, within the framework of a common language, millions of individual acts of building will together generate a town which is alive, and whole, and unpredictable, without control—this is the slow emergence of the quality without a name, as if from nothing.*" (p. 493). The book's "deepest and the most far-reaching postulate" is that all the large-scale order of a town can come from piecemeal acts, with no master plan, as a hand forms with no blueprint. Large patterns emerge as the end product of many small transformations, steered by people's shared awareness of the larger pattern and by incentives. This needs a nested hierarchy of groups (person, family, cluster, neighbourhood, community, town), each owning its common land and each responsible for helping the next level's patterns. The result is certain in kind and unpredictable in detail, like an oak. Examples: the hand and the bushes outside his window (pp. 497–498), CITY COUNTRY FINGERS grown by incentives at the bulges (pp. 501–502), a PROMENADE and a MAIN GATEWAY emerging from neighbourhood acts (pp. 502–505).

- "the postulate that it is possible for all the large-scale order of a town to be created purely by means of incremental piecemeal acts." (p. 496)
- "It cannot be created by decision. It cannot be designed. It cannot be predicted in a plan." (p. 510)

**26. Its ageless character** (pp. 511–528). Summary: "*And as the whole emerges, we shall see it take that ageless character which gives the timeless way its name. This character is a specific, morphological character, sharp and precise, which must come into being any time a building or a town becomes alive: it is the physical embodiment, in buildings, of the quality without a name.*" (p. 511). Buildings made this way look like the buildings of all traditional cultures, not from nostalgia but because that is the morphology of things correctly fitted to their forces. He lists its marks: the recurring patterns; greater differentiation; edges that are places with thickness; a balance of order and disorder; and parts that overlap and interlock so that none can be "lifted out". Modern prismatic geometry is "the naive order, created by the childish search for order" (p. 526). Examples: drawings by lay users of pattern languages (pp. 517–518), the prefabricated window versus the window with columns, splayed reveals and a seat continuous with the sill (pp. 522–523), the *Mustard Seed Garden Manual of Painting* (pp. 526–527).

- "It comes simply from the fact that every part is whole in its own right." (p. 522)
- "There are no gaps between the parts, because each gap is just as much a part itself." (p. 523)
- "The timeless character of buildings is as much a part of nature as the character of rivers, trees, hills, flames, and stars." (p. 528)
