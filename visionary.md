# Alexander the visionary

Most people who know Christopher Alexander know him for *A Pattern Language*, or for the way his patterns were taken up by software designers. That is a small part of him. Over fifty years, building and teaching all the while, he worked his way to a picture of the world in which beauty and goodness are facts, life is something space itself has in degrees, and the self each of us feels at our core is connected to something present in all matter. He arrived at these claims as an architect trying to explain what he saw on building sites, and he defended them as a scientist would, by observation he argued anyone could repeat.

Rufus Pollock, who started this wiki, reads him as a pioneer of a "Second Renaissance": on this reading, Alexander kept reason and observation while refusing the modern view that value is private opinion, and tried to show by careful comparison that some places and things really are more alive than others. This page sets out the main lines of that vision, mostly in his own words.

## Value is a matter of fact

The starting point, from *The Timeless Way of Building* on, is that judgments of value can be true or false. When people compare two doors, streets or cups and ask which has more life, they mostly agree, if they answer from feeling rather than fashion or theory. In 1979 he reports this agreement; by *The Phenomenon of Life* (2002) he makes it the basis of a different idea of truth.

> However, in the domain of patterns, where people seem to agree 90, 95, even 99 percent of the time, we may treat this agreement as an extraordinary, almost shattering, discovery, about the solidity of human feelings, and we may certainly use it as scientific.
>
> — *The Timeless Way of Building*, ch. 15, "The reality of patterns", p. 294

> In the world-view I am presenting, a second kind of statement is also considered capable of being true or false. These are statements about relative degree of life, degree of harmony, or degree of wholeness — in short, statements about value. In the view I hold, these statements about relative wholeness are also factual, and are the essential statements. They play a more fundamental role than statements about mechanisms.
>
> — *The Phenomenon of Life*, Preface, p. 17

His instrument for these judgments is the person. He asks which of two things is more like a picture of your own whole self, and treats the answers as observations (see [the mirror of the self](/concepts/mirror-of-the-self)).

## Life is a property of space, in degrees

Where biology defines life only for organisms, Alexander proposes that every part of the world has some degree of life. The degree depends on [wholeness](/concepts/wholeness-and-centers): on how far the centers in a place help one another.

> *What we call "life" is a general condition which exists, to some degree or other, in every part of space: brick, stone, grass, river, painting, building, daffodil, human being, forest, city. And further: The key to this idea is that every part of space — every connected region of space, small or large — has some degree of life, and that this degree of life is well defined, objectively existing, and measurable.*
>
> — *The Phenomenon of Life*, ch. 2, "Degrees of Life", p. 77

In the fourth book he presses the point. This life is not a pleasant feeling laid over function: it shows up in how well things work, and he finds that correlation hard to explain mechanically.

> It is indicative of *actual* life, of *practical, functional* life. It includes the way a parking lot works. ... It even includes the practical efficiency of the entrance to a house and the structural efficiency of the Golden Gate Bridge. What is there in the way things are hooked up in the universe, that can explain such a deeply surprising correlation? *Why should the practical beauty and efficiency of a girder in a bridge have anything to do with I?*
>
> — *The Luminous Ground*, Mid-Book Appendix, "Recapitulation of the Argument", p. 141

He thinks the idea that life is an attribute of space itself as startling as Maxwell's claim that light is a vibration of space, and argues it is needed to make his recursive account of centers consistent.

## Two worlds in our minds

Alexander believed that the trouble with modern building is at root a trouble with our picture of matter. If matter is inert and value only taste, there is nothing for a builder to be right about. He takes up Whitehead's name for the split, the "bifurcation of nature".

> There are thus two worlds in our minds. One is the scientific world which has been pictured through a highly complex system of mechanisms. The other is the world we actually experience. These two worlds, so far, have not been connected in a meaningful fashion. ... Whitehead believed that we will not have a proper grasp of the universe and our place in it, until the self which we experience in ourselves, and the machinelike character of matter we see outside ourselves, *can be united in a single picture*. I believe this.
>
> — *The Luminous Ground*, ch. 1, "Our Present Picture of the Universe", p. 13

He does not reject the mechanical picture but adds to it: Descartes's method for what can be treated as a machine, and the observer's own feeling of wholeness, shared and repeatable, where wholeness is the question. If both are used together, he claims, the bifurcation "is then solved" (p. 343).

## The "I" and the luminous ground

Here Alexander goes furthest. If people across cultures agree about which things are most like their own self, and if waves, snowdrops and foxes have the same quality as the best buildings, then the likeness cannot simply be something artists put in. He concludes that each living center is connected to a single self-like ground, which he calls "the I".

> What I call "the I" is that interior element in a work of art, or in a work of nature, which makes one feel related to it. It may occur in a leaf, or in a picture, in a house, in a wave, even in a grain of sand, or in an ornament. It is not ego. It is not me. It is not individual at all, having to do with me, or you. It is humble, and enormous: that thing in common which each one of us has in us. It is the spirit which animates each living center.
>
> — *The Luminous Ground*, Preface, p. 2

> I suggest that, so long as space/matter remains undifferentiated, the I which stands behind it remains incommunicado, not reachable, not connected with the matter. It becomes connected with matter — and visible to us — only as centers form. The stronger a center forms, the more it becomes window-like, and the more it allows us to see the I.
>
> — *The Luminous Ground*, ch. 6, "The Blazing One", p. 151

On this view a [center](/concepts/centers) is a window, and its degree of life is how far the window is open. He places the idea beside the mystical traditions, from Sufi writers to Eckhart, but insists he offers it "in a scientific spirit", believing that "some concept along these lines is necessary as a part of physics" (p. 156).

## Making as healing

The vision is not only about what we look at. It is about what happens to us when we make things. Alexander noticed, in himself and his students, that making something with life leaves a person calmer and more whole for days, and that making something dead does the opposite.

> *People are deeply nourished by the process of creating wholeness.* If a person dedicates himself to making the field of centers, to making things whole, the more he or she does it, the more whole he or she becomes within him- or herself.
>
> — *The Luminous Ground*, ch. 9, "Making Wholeness Heals the Maker", p. 263

He recalls Marx's account of alienated labour and notes that most building workers work "mainly or entirely for the money"; making living centers, he argues, nourishes the world and the worker together.

## What this means for how we live and build

The strangest consequence, and the one he says he understood last, is that the personal and the objective meet. If each maker works towards what truly touches their own self, the result is not a jumble of private tastes but a world that works.

> ... a world constructed in the most personal and individual fashion, made by people who are searching deeply to follow the nature of their own true I, their own true selves, will be — in the most public, objective, and universal sense — a world which is functional, adequate, and harmonious.
>
> — *The Luminous Ground*, Mid-Book Appendix, "Recapitulation of the Argument", p. 142

For a builder this means working step by step, testing each move against feeling (see [structure-preserving transformations](/concepts/structure-preserving-transformations)). For everyone it means that the places we make are not neutral: they help or hinder us in being whole.

## Where he is contested

His evidence for agreement comes mostly from his own classes and experiments, with few independent replications. And on what the agreement shows, Alexander himself never settles the question. In *The Luminous Ground* he sets out two versions of his hypothesis, one psychological and one physical, and says he cannot choose between them, though his instinct favours the physical one. On either reading, he says, the practical picture stands:

> Whether the humane, psychological and positivistic view, or the more dream-like plenum view is more accurate, I do not know. But the overall picture I am trying to present in either case remains. One way or the other, the ground of I is real. Either the ground is a psychological description of the way we *experience* living structure. Or it is a factually supportable, previously unknown aspect of matter.
>
> — *The Luminous Ground*, ch. 6, "The Blazing One", p. 153

He ends the four books with the same honesty. The positivist in him, formed at Cambridge, still doubts; the scientist who follows the evidence, having ruled out every other account he could construct, concludes it must be true.

> It is something like this that I hang onto. I want to believe it. I cannot believe it. But I believe it must be true.
>
> — *The Luminous Ground*, Epilogue, "Empirical Certainty and Enduring Doubt", p. 344

## Further reading

- [*The Luminous Ground*](/works/2004-the-luminous-ground): the fourth book of *The Nature of Order*, where the "I", the ground and the modified picture of matter are set out. The Mid-Book Appendix (pp. 135–142) is the clearest short summary of the whole argument.
- [*The Phenomenon of Life*](/works/2002-the-phenomenon-of-life): the first book, on life, wholeness, centers and the mirror of the self.
- [*The Timeless Way of Building*](/works/1979-the-timeless-way-of-building): the 1979 statement of the "quality without a name".
- Concepts: [the quality without a name](/concepts/quality-without-a-name), [deep feeling](/concepts/deep-feeling), [not-separateness](/concepts/not-separateness).
- Catherine Tran and Rosie Bell, ["Christopher Alexander on matter and meaning"](https://seedsofrenaissance.org/blog/christopher-alexander) (2025): an essay on Alexander's challenge to the "ultra-mechanistic" worldview.
