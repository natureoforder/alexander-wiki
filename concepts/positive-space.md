# Positive space

Trace the outside of a bowl with your eye, from its rim down to the table. Then attend to the wedge of air beside it. The same curve gives shape to the bowl on one side and the air on the other. In *The Phenomenon of Life* Alexander makes this observation about the Kizaemon tea bowl. He even draws the space beside the vessel so the reader can see it as a figure in its own right.[^1]

Alexander calls space **positive** when it has a discernible shape and coherence of its own. In a building, considerable care may have gone into the rooms while the passage connecting them pinches or splays wherever their corners dictate. The passage is a place in which someone stands, turns, or passes a neighbor. Its shape matters as much as the shape of the rooms. In Alexander’s terms each can become a [center](wholeness-and-centers.md) that helps give form to its neighbors.[^2]

## Read a city as a drawing

Alexander reproduces a detail from Giambattista Nolli’s plan of Rome. Follow the open space along a street and into a square. Its outline is as recognizable as the outlines of the adjoining buildings. Certain public interiors appear in the same light tone as the street, so the route through the drawing can continue indoors. Alexander uses the plan to show what happens when shaping one part of a city also gives definition to an adjacent part.[^3]

## Try it outside

Sketch a courtyard or the space beside your front door. Shade the buildings. On another sheet shade the open ground instead. Which portions would be easy to give directions to? Where would you suggest waiting for a friend? If one corner trails away into a service strip, test a small change on tracing paper. See what happens to the shape on **both** sides of the line you move.

The earlier pattern [Positive Outdoor Space](../patterns/positive-outdoor-space.md) takes up a particular design problem at the scale of a garden or a square. Positive space is the more general property.
