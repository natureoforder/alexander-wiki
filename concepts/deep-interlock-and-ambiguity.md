# Deep interlock and ambiguity

Some edges bring two places so thoroughly into one another that either place helps explain the shape of the other. A covered market stall opening onto a square is an everyday example: the strip under the awning can belong both to the stall and to the public gathering place.[^1]

Draw the market square once including the covered strip and once leaving it out. If both drawings capture something real about the square, the ambiguity may be part of what makes its edge work.

[^1]: Christopher Alexander, *The Nature of Order*, Book One, *The Phenomenon of Life*, chapter 5, pp. 195–199.
