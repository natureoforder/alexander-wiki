# Making a living center

At the West Dean Visitor’s Centre in England, the builders tried cardboard columns at full size. Christopher Alexander was surprised by how low the top of a column wanted to be. The capital came almost level with a visitor’s face. The arch rose from there, leaving room to pass beneath its middle. At the side you could stand close enough to see and touch the capital. Lowering one element had changed the intimacy of the room.[^1]

This is what Alexander is after when he asks a maker to give a center **life**. A column may already do its structural job. Its dimensions also help determine what it feels like to occupy the space beside it, how the arch overhead appears from the room, and whether the two feel as though they belong together. At West Dean the builders could vary the dimensions before committing to them. On the slab they also placed chairs to find the position and angle of windows looking toward the view.[^2]

## What happens to the maker?

Later in Book Four, Alexander asks why the experience of making a place whole can stay with someone for days. He recalls Barbara Winslow, a student in a 1977 seminar, who kept a diary of what happened to her while working on drawings and paintings. She described an unfamiliar peacefulness. Alexander recognizes in her account something he had felt even after a modest adjustment that finally worked. For him the connection runs both ways. A person gives shape to a place, and the quality achieved in the place can change how that person feels.[^3]

When you next adjust something by hand, try noticing when you stop. What tells you the chair, lamp, or picture finally sits well with its surroundings? Can you show somebody else what changed? [Wholeness and centers](wholeness-and-centers.md)
