# The mirror of the self

On a café table sit an old-fashioned glass salt shaker and a bottle of ketchup. Alexander asks a peculiar question. If you had to take one of the two as a portrait of yourself, which would you choose? Consider the parts of yourself you usually keep out of a portrait, too. After readers have made their choice, he discusses how differently they might have answered a question about which object they found fashionable or familiar.[^1]

He calls the comparison **the mirror of the self**. His proposal is that someone’s felt response to a pair of objects can reveal something about the objects’ relative [wholeness](wholeness-and-centers.md). A personal question thus becomes his proposed instrument of observation. In his own trials, he reports that more than four in five chose the salt shaker. That figure describes his example. Whether others make similar judgments about another pair is a question to investigate anew.[^2]

## Try it with two doorways

Choose two entrances you regularly pass. Give each a little time. Imagine you have to pick one to stand for the whole person you are, including qualities strangers seldom see. Write down your choice before explaining it. If someone is with you, have them do the same before you compare answers.

Now return to the doorways. Where did your eye settle? Which details did the other person notice? Would you have picked differently if asked which entrance looked more expensive, or which you would photograph? This is a wiki exercise using Alexander’s comparison. In the book he offers further pairs, including spoons and views of streets, for readers who want to test their responses against his.[^3]

The comparison offers a way into the older question of what we mean when we describe a place as good. [The quality without a name](quality-without-a-name.md)

[^1]: Christopher Alexander, *The Phenomenon of Life*, ch. 8, pp. 316–319. The pictured objects appear on p. 317.

[^2]: *The Phenomenon of Life*, ch. 8, pp. 314–318. Alexander gives his reported result for the salt shaker and discusses the reasoning of people who chose the bottle on p. 317.

[^3]: *The Phenomenon of Life*, ch. 8, pp. 320–323. The pairs extend the comparison from the scale of things that fit in a hand to the scale of buildings and streets.
