# Wholeness

At his family’s house in West Sussex, Alexander describes the short trip from the kitchen, over the flagstones, to cut roses. The terrace was also where the family sat. Its position at the kitchen door, with the lawn and flower beds in reach, gave it a part in the day that its size and material alone would tell you little about.[^1]

**Wholeness** is Alexander’s name for the structure a place already has through the arrangement and relative strength of its [centers](centers.md). The kitchen, terrace and beds each have a character of their own. Their particular arrangement also brings a larger place into focus. Ask someone who knows the house where they go to cut flowers, and they can trace it for you.[^2]

Try the same question at a familiar doorway. What happens there over a day? Does someone put their shopping down, stay to talk, or step outside to catch the last of the sun? Sketch the territory each action occupies. For a way to see how even one mark changes the structure around it, try the paper experiment in [Wholeness and centers](wholeness-and-centers.md).

[^1]: Christopher Alexander, *The Nature of Order*, Book One, *The Phenomenon of Life*, ch. 3, pp. 93–95. The garden at Meadow Lodge is pictured on p. 94.

[^2]: *The Phenomenon of Life*, ch. 3, pp. 83–85, on the relation between a whole configuration and the centers one can distinguish within it. On p. 95 Alexander traces centers through the house, garden and lane.
