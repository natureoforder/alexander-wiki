# Who can change a design on site?

Christopher Alexander once visited fifteen acres in Mendham Township, New Jersey, where a client hoped to build about a dozen houses. The undergrowth was so thick that he could barely get through. Where should the first house go? To answer that question he needed to see more of the ground. Clearing even a little of it would itself settle some design questions. A tree taken out to improve the view of the site might have made a fine edge to a garden.[^1]

Alexander imagined alternating between selective clearing and decisions about gardens, buildings and lot lines. A promising opening might admit sun to a house in a position nobody had foreseen on paper. To take advantage of it, someone present during the clearing would need authority to alter the siting. The example describes the process he thought the land required. It leaves the eventual fate of the houses open.[^2]

## Where the decision can still move

A different problem arose in a proposal for marble floors in the Athens Megaron. The installation window in Greece was short. Alexander’s team planned a workspace in California where they could lay out floor sections at full size and judge the colors and shapes together. Pieces on numbered mats could still be changed before shipment. The crucial resource here was time with the assembled work and the ability to act on what became visible. The team made an eighteen-inch sample. The chapter distinguishes the sample from the proposed production sequence.[^3]

For a project of your own, choose one decision that depends on seeing two things together. Which pieces must be in place for that judgment? Who can still move them then?
