# Concepts

How can two places serve the same purpose, yet one feels so much better to be in? And if we can recognize the difference, how do we make it? Those questions run from *The Timeless Way of Building* through all four books of *The Nature of Order*.

Start with [Wholeness and centers](wholeness-and-centers.md) for Alexander’s account of what we see, and [Generative codes](generative-codes.md) for how that changes the order in which we build. To follow a team making decisions on the ground, open the [practical guides](../practice/index.md).

## Learning to see

- **The quality without a name.** What does “good” mean when it describes a place? Alexander tries a series of ordinary words, among them *alive*, *whole*, and *free*. Each picks out something recognizable. Each also fits some places that lack the quality he has in mind. Start with the examples in *The Timeless Way of Building*, chapter 2, pp. 25–39, then turn to *The Phenomenon of Life* (Book One), chapter 2, pp. 63–78, where he proposes that places can possess different degrees of life.
- **Wholeness and centers.** A cyclist stops under a roadside tree. What does the cyclist find there that a drawing of the tree and road could leave out? Book One, chapter 3, pp. 91–95. [Read the concept entry](wholeness-and-centers.md)
- **The fifteen properties.** Alexander offers a vocabulary for describing how one center supports another. Start with *levels of scale* (Book One, chapter 5, pp. 145–150), then *boundaries* (pp. 158–164). Compare the surround of a doorway with *Building Edge*, pattern 160. A threshold can itself be a place someone occupies.
- **Positive space.** Try tracing the outdoor shape between two buildings without drawing the buildings themselves. Does it have a shape you can recognize? Read Book One, chapter 5, beginning on p. 173, alongside *Positive Outdoor Space* (106) and *Courtyards Which Live* (115). Both appear in the selections made for the Petit Bois Martin architects. [Pattern index](../patterns/index.md) · [Petit Bois Martin selections](https://github.com/natureoforder/pattern-language/blob/main/shortlist.md)
- **The mirror of the self.** Alexander asks people to compare two things and consider which one better expresses their own deepest nature. His salt shaker and ketchup bottle make a disarmingly ordinary pair on which to try the question. Read Book One, chapter 8, pp. 315–323, including the instructions that precede the pictured pairs.

## Deciding what to make next

- **Structure-preserving transformations.** How could adding something new make the structure already there easier to see? Compare *The Process of Creating Life* (Book Two), chapter 2, pp. 51–84, with chapter 9, pp. 249–266. The first names a kind of change. The second asks how to judge an individual step by its effect on the whole.
- **Patterns, sequences, and generative codes.** A pattern can describe the relation between a garden and the building beside it. A sequence tells you which decisions to make first. A neighborhood code must also establish who takes those decisions and how people work together. Read Book Two, chapter 11, pp. 307–316, and chapter 13, pp. 341–368. [Generative codes](generative-codes.md) · [The authors’ account of the three forms](https://www.livingneighborhoods.org/ht-0/patternlanguages.htm)
- **Deep feeling.** How might a quality that people can sense together guide a decision before anyone can draw a final plan? Read Book Two, chapter 14, pp. 369–396, then compare *A Vision of a Living World* (Book Three), chapter 8, pp. 259–282, on forming a collective vision. Book Two returns to the Eishin campus on pp. 394 and 424–426. [How the Eishin campus was laid out](../practice/eishin-campus.md) · [Eishin reading notes and pictures](https://github.com/natureoforder/pattern-language/issues/3)

## Making the work possible

- **Who can change a design on site?** A contractor might discover a better arrangement after the plans are agreed. Whether anyone can act on it depends on the contracts, the money and the order of decisions. Read Book Two, chapter 19, pp. 511–530, alongside Book Three, chapter 17, pp. 561–578, on organizing large projects. [The original question about contractors](https://github.com/natureoforder/nature-of-order/issues/1)
- **A living center and the experience of making it.** In *The Luminous Ground* (Book Four), Alexander describes the arches and columns of West Dean while asking what it means for a made thing to take on a presence of its own. Read chapter 5, beginning with the West Dean passage on p. 118 and the column design on p. 126. Chapter 9, pp. 261–270, turns the question around and asks what the act of making does to the maker.

The opening questions follow Rufus Pollock’s [overview of The Nature of Order](https://github.com/natureoforder/nature-of-order/issues/2). His [notes on The Timeless Way of Building](https://github.com/natureoforder/pattern-language/blob/main/Timeless%20Way%20Notes.md) pair the question of goodness with particular courtyards and windows.
