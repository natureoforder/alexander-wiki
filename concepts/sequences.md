# Sequences

At the Julian Street Inn, the designers settled the positions of two courtyards before they placed the dining hall. The courtyards gave them something to work with. They could now ask where the hall would serve both outdoor spaces and what shape it could take there. In Alexander’s account, reversing those decisions would have made it harder to give the courtyards useful shapes.[^1]

A **generative sequence** orders decisions so that each step creates the conditions for the next. Alexander offers a way to feel this happening. Ask someone to read aloud his sequence for a Japanese tea house while you imagine a particular site. Early steps establish the seclusion of the garden and distinguish its outer and inner parts. By the time you reach the low entrance to the building, you can imagine approaching it along the path. Only later do you arrange the mats and hearth inside.[^2]

To examine a sequence, choose two steps and exchange them. If you had to fix the entrance while you still knew nothing about the approach, what would you have to guess? For a sequence intended for use across many different sites, see [Generative codes](generative-codes.md).

[^1]: Christopher Alexander, *The Nature of Order*, Book Two, *The Process of Creating Life*, ch. 11, pp. 300–301. Alexander returns to the Julian Street Inn to explain why the courtyards had to come early.

[^2]: *The Process of Creating Life*, ch. 11, section 5, pp. 302–304. Follow the twenty-four steps from the garden to the tea room.
