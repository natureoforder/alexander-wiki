# Structure-preserving transformations

Draw a circle. Before you add anything, you can already point to its middle. Put a small dark spot there. What was implicit in the outline is now visible in the ink. You have changed the figure quite substantially, yet someone looking at the result can see where the change came from. Alexander uses this drawing to introduce a **structure-preserving transformation**. Take an arrangement as it stands, find a center it already suggests, and give that center a stronger form.[^1]

## A square across centuries

How could a series of builders work on St Mark’s Square in Venice without anyone at the beginning knowing what the finished square would look like? In Book Two, Alexander invites the reader to follow the diagrams across each row. One shows a particular stage in the square’s history. The next identifies a place that was already beginning to take shape. Further drawings show what the new construction did there. The next builder inherited a different square and a different set of possibilities. This is Alexander’s reading of the development, reconstructed after the fact.[^2]

Try the same question at the scale of a room. Imagine a window recess where someone often drags a chair to read. A seat fitted into the recess could strengthen a place the room already offers. Before deciding on its depth, walk past it and sit down. Does it give the reader enough room? Does it still leave the way through the room easy to use? [Window Place](../patterns/window-place.md)

[^1]: Christopher Alexander, *The Nature of Order*, Book Two, *The Process of Creating Life*, ch. 2, pp. 52–54. The first drawings appear on p. 52.

[^2]: *The Process of Creating Life*, ch. 9, pp. 252–255. The sequence on pp. 253–254 pairs historical configurations with Alexander’s interpretation of their latent centers.
