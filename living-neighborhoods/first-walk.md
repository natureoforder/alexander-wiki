# A first neighborhood walk

Imagine that your block wants to make a small square. One neighbor proposes a bench beside a wall. Another remembers that this is where a delivery driver turns every morning. A walk gives both observations a place on the same drawing, while the position of the bench is still an open question.

**Before you leave**, draw or print a map that everyone can recognize. Include entrances, paths, trees, and the edges of buildings. Make it large enough to write on while standing outdoors. The authors show how they approached this at several scales. [Making a base map](https://www.livingneighborhoods.org/actions/basemap.htm)

**On the walk**, pause where someone has something specific to show the group. Mark the spot and record what happens there. “Children wait here in the shade after school” gives you more to work with than “keep this tree.” Leave room for a second account of the same place. [The authors’ introduction to diagnosis](https://www.livingneighborhoods.org/actions/diagnosis-intro.htm)

**Back at the table**, pick one proposed change and trace who would pass through it and what it would disturb. If the answer depends on the time of day, mark that question on the map and go back when you can observe it. The shared map gives the next decision a starting point. [The diagnosis phase](https://www.livingneighborhoods.org/packets/diagnosis-phase.htm)

Alexander calls the ordered instructions through which a place takes shape a *generative code*. What a group discovers on its first walk can change which step makes sense next. [Generative codes](../concepts/generative-codes.md) · [Living Neighborhoods](index.md)
